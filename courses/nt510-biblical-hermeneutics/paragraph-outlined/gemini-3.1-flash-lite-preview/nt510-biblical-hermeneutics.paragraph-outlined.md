### I. Introduction

#### A. Definition of Biblical Hermeneutics
The purpose of our course is to introduce students to a brief history of the English Bible. Nowhere in the curriculum before we organized the hermeneutics class, was there a place where every student would be getting an introduction to the history of the English Bible. It seemed incredible that a person could graduate from seminary without having some overview of, “How did we get this English Bible of ours?” So we will begin that. It is also a helpful introduction to the issue of hermeneutics. We start tonight with a brief history of the English Bible and then we are going to seek to understand the goal of interpretation, what part presuppositions play in interpretation, the role of genre in interpretation, how to arrive at the meaning of an ancient text, as well as the present significance. The major goal of this course will be to master the technical hermeneutical vocabulary found in R. H. Stein’s, A Basic Guide to Interpreting the Bible.

#### B. Importance of Biblical Hermeneutics
There are three texts: Klein, Blomberg, and Hubbard, “Introduction to Biblical Interpretation”. That was out of print for a while, but it is in print again; the second is “Stein’s Basic Guide to Interpreting the Bible”; and the other is Paul Wegner, “The Journey from Text to Translation.” That is a really good text. It fills in a lot of things in this area that I could not find elsewhere. A lot of books have been written on hermeneutics in the last 15 years. It is simply incredible to try to keep up with them. I think I can honestly say that of all the books I have read so far, there is none I agree with more than the “Stein” one, and that is why we are going to be using it. I think you will find it very user-friendly and helpful.

By the end of this class, you will have an understanding of hermeneutics that will make clear just what interpreting the Bible is all about, an understanding you did not have before. I guarantee that. The best part of it will be to master a vocabulary of definitions. There will be 12 terms or so, expressions that we will carefully define. That will become the framework in which we will understand the issues and discuss them. I will give definitions to these terms and there are other definitions that you can find elsewhere. But in this class, we will always use the same definitions. It is kind of silly to talk about meanings when you have a different understanding of what that term means, and you talk at cross purposes. So we will have a specific vocabulary that we will use precisely time and time again and it will become second nature. You will find that will be very, very helpful.

By taking this course, you will arrive at an understanding of how the Bible was translated into the English language. We will talk about some of the various translations, their strengths, and weaknesses. We will develop a conceptual framework in vocabulary, which explains the role of the author and reader. We will distinguish different genres of Biblical literature and understand the basic rules involved in that in those particular genres. Similarly, you don’t interpret a medical report the same way you interpret a love poem. The Bible is filled with different kinds of genres and there are different rules that the editors expected people would understand about them. But over the millennia, we have lost sight of some of the rules and some of the principles of these genres and we will re-learn them in class. We will seek to understand how to interpret various approaches.

### II. Early Beginnings of Biblical Interpretation

#### A. Jewish Interpretation before the Babylonian Exile
I want to talk about the translation of the Bible into English. We have to remember, of course, that there was no such thing as an English language when the Bible was written. Actually, English is an amalgam of various kinds of dialects and the English language begins roughly in the 5th century when Germanic tribes left the continent of Europe and came to England. The main three tribes were the Angles, from which we get England; the Saxons, these were a Germanic group from the Holstein region; and the Jutes. The Jutes were out of Denmark. If any of you are history buffs, in World War I the greatest naval battle in the world was the Battle of Jutland – the British Naval Forces and the German Navy slugged it out with one another. The Battle of Jutland was off of Denmark, the Jutland Peninsula.

#### B. Jewish Interpretation during the Babylonian Exile
As they came to England, they were not taking vacation cruises, they came to conquer, and they did. As they settled in England, there developed a common dialect out of these Germanic tribes which was called Anglo-Saxon or Old English. If any of you have studied German, you will note that a lot of the vocabulary in German is very, very similar. I have listed some of them: House, spelled differently, pronounced the same way; shoe; glass of water; book; finger; knee; glass; hand; fire; boat, same; blue; white; road; student. So, lots of words are similar. The reason is very clear. English comes out in part from the German language.

#### C. Jewish Interpretation after the Babylonian Exile
In the 11th century William the Conqueror, who was a Norman in France, conquered the English in 1066 at the Battle of Hastings, introduced the longbow at that time and brought magnificent victory to the French. It was kind of a reverse of D-Day in 1944. Here was the Normandy people going over to England, rather than the reverse. That brought then a Norman or French invasion and the influence of the French language came. The French language worked with the Anglo-Saxon. You throw a little Latin in there and put it in a whirring blender, you get English, our English. So that is the way the English language originated. In the earliest period, the Bible of the people of England was the Latin Vulgate. It is the Bible of the Church. Most people could not read, however. In fact, a lot of the priests could not read. So, in effect, people learned their Christianity from the art of the church, the paintings of the church. I don’t know if you have gone to any of the old cathedrals in Europe. Stained glass windows. These were pages of the Bible, so to speak. You would learn your Bible stories through them. The preaching was lousy, look to the windows, and you read your Bible this way, or something like that. You would go to a door in a church and there were carvings of Bible stories all over the door. Anything that was art was primarily associated with the art of the Christian Faith and many people learned their Bible from these things. There were other ways people learned about the Christian Faith, not only the preaching of the church and so forth. There were groups called troubadours, people who would have ability to sing and go from village to village, and they would sing, and people would learn the Bible. Some troubadour would come to your little town and he would start singing, “Only a boy named David, only a little sling. Only a boy named David, but oh, how he could sing”, and they would sing the story about David. We do the same with our children. A cool way of learning, especially for people who can’t read or write, like our children at that age. They can learn stories, but they can’t read or write. “Zacchaeus was a wee little man, a wee little man was he” and so forth. They would sing the Bible stories and this is the way people would learn them.

### III. New Testament Interpretation in Early Christianity

#### A. The Use of the Old Testament in Early Christian Interpretation
In the Old English dialect, beginning at around the 600s, the Bible began to be translated into Anglo-Saxon, not English, but Old English, the pre-French influenced English. Sedman around the 7th century put into poetry and song the Biblical stories. Aldhelm, who died in 709, translated The Psalms into Anglo-Saxon, the Lord’s prayer, the Decalogue. Interesting. Psalms, you need to sing, that is what you sing. The Lord’s prayer, you have to pray together. And the Decalogue, moral instructions, those basic needs of the Church were first dealt with. They didn’t start out translating the book of Leviticus into Anglo-Saxon, but those which were most crucial to the worship of the Church. Then Venerable Bede by his death, the Gospels have been translated into Anglo-Saxon. It is debated as to how much he personally translated, but he was very much responsible for seeing that it was translated. But this is all Old English or Anglo-Saxon.

#### B. The Interpretation of Jesus' Teachings
The first real approach to translating the Bible into English would be William of Shoreham, 1325, who translated the various psalms into English, not Old English, but English. The first major name, however, who comes across in the history of the English Bible is a man named John Wycliffe. This is the Old English kind of spelling. If you look at a Bible back in the 1600, 1700, they will spell it in this manner. John Wycliffe produced the first Bible in the English language in 1382. He really was a kind of pre-Luther/Calvin reformer. He convinced a lot of people who were priestly and these priests would go around sharing the Bible and the message of the Bible with other people. He did not know Greek or Hebrew, so what he did was to translate the Bible from the language he did know which it was in, and that was the Latin Vulgate. So the Wycliffe translation was a translation of the Vulgate, which was a translation of the Greek and Hebrew. You say, that’s not real good, you should go from the Greek and the Hebrew. If Wycliffe were here, he would say, “What do you want, nothing? Or from the Vulgate”. The answer is sure, anything, we don’t have anything, so the Vulgate was really fine. As people began to hear the Scriptures read, they began to notice a lot of things which caused problems and that is, that the character of the clergy and a lot of the political leaders did not match what they were reading in the Bible, and they became critical of the church. They became critical of their political leaders as well. The result was that the ecclesiastic or similar authorities began to cramp out on this and said, “Let’s put an end to this” and by 1414, it then became a capital offense to be found reading the Bible in the English language. For just reading the Bible in the English language, you could get executed. I wonder how many people in our churches would be reading the Bible if that was the alternative. But they did that. With Wycliffe it was too late because Wycliffe died in 1384, some 30 years earlier; so what they did was to dig up his body and then they burned his bones at the stake. We laugh at that, but there has been a recent survey among translators and well over 98% said this is the way they prefer. This translation occurs before the printing press. So they are handwritten copies and there are handwritten sections of the Bible that the Lollards were selling. They were like Bible booksellers who went around selling passages and teaching from those passages. The result was that if you were going to start a mission society whose basic goal was to translate the Bible into the language of the people and you were living in and coming out of the English-speaking world, you might think of the name Wycliffe Bible Translators, because he was the first to really begin the translation of the Bible into English. He did the whole Bible that way.

#### C. The Interpretation of Paul's Writings
The next person who comes along comes after the printing press. His name is William Tyndale. He produces the first printed New Testament. Remember, Wycliffe has already done the whole Bible, but it is handwritten. This is the first printed New Testament, it appears in 1526. It was not published in England because there was opposition to this. It was printed in Worms, Germany, the place where the reformation was centered and very active. He did something in translating the New Testament that revealed right away that he was a Luther supporter. Tactically, that might not have been a wise thing to do because Henry VIII, the King of England, had received a medal from the Pope for resisting the Lutheran doctrine in England. During a meeting later on, on opening his New Testament, he noticed there was kind of a Lutheran translation. It was evident because in the introduction, the preface, he used the term, “justification” and talked about the need for justification. He used other words that were not the Church’s words. He used the word, “repentance” instead of “doing penance”. He used the word, “congregation” instead of “church”. He used the word, “elder” rather than “priest”. But I think the clearest indication of all was his order of the New Testament. Luther has an unusual order in his New Testament. The last four books of the Lutheran New Testament are not 1, 2, 3 John, Jude, Revelation like we have, but Hebrews, James, Jude, Revelation. Very unusual order. I think it partly involved his evaluation of those books, he didn’t like some of them, James especially, and Revelation and the like. When the Tyndale New Testament came out, everybody knew this guy is pro-Luther because his order is the same. He also translated large parts of the Old Testament. Some were destroyed, some were burned as he was fleeing, and the result was that he never completely finished that. There was a lot of opposition to this and the result was that they sought to destroy the various new testaments. There is some irony in this because the Church would go about trying to collect Lutheran New Testaments and some of the merchants were approached and told, “We can get some of these New Testaments for you, we will collect them.” They were actually some supporters of Tyndale; so they were saying, “We can sell them, make profit and make more”. So they were selling to some of the clergy these New Testaments, making profit so they could make even more New Testaments and sell them this way. The attempt to destroy the Tyndale New Testament was really very effective. There were something like 18,000 printed New Testaments. Only two still remain, one in the British Library in London and the other in the Baptist Bible College in London. Listen to how some of the religious leaders oppose this. The Bishop of London fumed against the maintainers of Luther’s sect that have craftily translated the New Testament into our English language. Cardinal Wolsey assured the people that no burnt offering could be more pleasing to Almighty God than the burning of a Tyndale New Testament. Another man by the name of Cochlea says, “The New Testament translated into the Vulgate tongue, meaning the common everyday language, is in truth the food of death, the fuel of sin, the veil of malice, the pretext of false liberty, the protection of disobedience, the corruption of discipline, the depravity of morals, the termination of concord, the death of honesty, the wellspring of vices, the disease of virtues, the instigation of rebellion, the milk of pride, the nourishment of contempt, the death of peace, and destruction of charity, the enemy of unity, the murderer of truth. If you read very carefully between the lines, you get the impression he didn’t like it very much. So there was this attempt to wipe out the Tyndale New Testaments. Tyndale himself was living at the time in Antwerp, Belgium, which was an open city, neither Catholic nor Protestant. He was kidnapped by followers of Henry VIII and in 1536 in the little town named Vilvoorde outside of Brussels, he was burned at the stake. His last words were, “Lord, open the eyes of the King of England.” If someone were about to strangle me or burn me, I don’t know if I would say that. I might have some other choice words. Who knows. God does promise that at such times the Spirit will be present and we will be able to do things like Stephen does: “Lord, lay not this sin to their charge” as he is being stoned, and blessed rather than cursed. A good example. Tyndale was an excellent translator. He had a mastery of the Greek and Hebrew languages. He not only knew the languages, the original languages, but he had a tremendous ability to translate that into good English. You say, “When I look at Tyndale’s writing, it sounds very archaic”. It wasn’t archaic in 1530, it was very modern. When the King James version is later translated in 1611, with regard to the New Testament, 90% of our King James version is simply Tyndale. He did a masterful, masterful job. A great translator. A great martyr of the Faith.

### IV. Conclusion

#### A. Summary of Early Beginnings of Biblical Interpretation
When he dies, his work is followed by a number of his coworkers. For instance, Miles Coverdale was one of his disciples and he completes and publishes a complete printed English Bible. Pay attention, this is the first printed Bible in completion. Wycliffe had a Bible, but it was handwritten. This is the first printed Bible in the English language in completion. He had been a coworker with Tyndale. He did not know Greek or Hebrew, however. What he did was some minor revisions of the New Testament and he did the Old Testament by taking that which Tyndale himself had already done and then the parts that he had to do for himself, he translated from the Latin Vulgate and from Luther’s German. The result was to come out with a Bible. He also was the first person up to that time who separated the books of the Apocrypha from the Old Testament. If you come from a Roman Catholic background, the books of the Apocrypha, 1, 2 Maccabbees, Tobit, Judith, are intermingled in the Old Testament. The reformers had to wrestle with the issue, what books belong in our Bible? We will talk more about that at the end of the semester when we talk about the canon of Scripture: Which books measure up to the canon of Scripture. They concluded for various reasons we will discuss then. The Apocrypha did not measure up and were not to be understood as Scripture. So they separated them out of the Old Testament, put them in between the Old Testament and the New Testament. English translations continued to have them in the Bible, between the Old and the New Testaments, indicating they were separate from those two, until sometime around the 1700s. Then there was a large, massive attempt in England to produce cheap Bibles so that everybody in England could read and have their own Bible. One way you could make it cheaper was to leave out the Apocrypha. Since they did not think this was part of Scripture, they did so from then on, there was a tendency not to have them in the Bible.

### Early Beginnings (Part 2)

#### 4. Thomas Matthew (John Rogers)
Coverdale mostly revised the work of Tyndale in the New Testament. Another translator who was a coworker with Tyndale was a man by the name of John Rogers, who produced the Matthew Bible, published in 1537. Why did he call it the Matthew Bible? John Rogers knew what happened to Tyndale, and he said, “Let them burn at the stake any Thomas Matthew they can find, just leave John Rogers alone”. Unfortunately, we will find that they found who he was, and he too was martyred for the Faith. This was a revision of Tyndale for the most part. He used Coverdale for those areas that Tyndale did not translate. There is an irony here in that in 1537 both Matthew’s translation and Coverdale’s were licensed by Henry VIII. In other words, they could be printed and sold in England, and this is only one year after the martyrdom of Tyndale. In 1555 Rogers was burned at the stake by Mary Tudor.

#### 5. Richard Taverner
Another translation, the Taverner. One comes out in 1539, a revision of Matthew’s Bible. All of these translations that we talk about will be revisions of Tyndale’s. They all come from that central root, that central stem, Tyndale’s work. Most of them always started out with the presupposition, “Unless it’s broke, let’s not try to fix it; is there any reason we should change Tyndale.” If they were working on say, Coverdale’s translation, they would say, “Is there any reason to change Coverdale?” which of course was Tyndale. So they keep on going back to the original parent, Tyndale.

#### 6. The Great Bible
The Great Bible, named for its size. I have listed here, its size was 15 inches by 9 inches. I have seen elsewhere it was 16.5 inches by 11 inches, which may be whether you leave out the blank margin around, or not. Whatever it was, it was not your pocket New Testament for witnessing on street corners. This was a chained Bible in the pulpit. This is the first authorized version. It is not THE authorized version. It was the first one authorized. But when we talk about THE authorized version, we are talking about the King James authorized version of 1611. This was authorized before by Henry VIII. He had assistance, support from Thomas Cromwell and Coverdale. What they did was to revise the Matthew Bible. Published in 1539, it was the official Bible of Henry VIII and his reign.

##### A. Authorization and Content
To get authorized, they did some things that would make it more palatable to the clergy and to political leadership. One was by going back to the traditional order of the New Testament books at the end. So they reversed the order and instead of following Luther, they followed the other ones before. It was also required that there be no footnotes in this Bible. You say, what is wrong with footnotes? I’ll show you what is wrong with some footnotes shortly. These are not the kind of footnotes you think of. A 1-cubit footnote (a cubit is about 18 inches) or 1 talent (a talent weighed this much). That is not what we mean by footnotes. In the Geneva Bible, which comes later, here are some of the kind of footnotes you get: “Here is wisdom. Let him that hath wit count the number of the beast, which is the number of a man, his number is 600, three-score and 6.” Footnote: “Such as may be understood by man’s reason, for about 660 years after this revelation, the Pope or antichrist began to be manifested in the world.” These are the kind of notes we’re talking about. You have another one: “There followed another angel saying, “It is fallen, it is fallen, Babylon the great city”: “Signifying Rome, for as much as the vices which were in Babylon are found in Rome in greater abundance, as persecution of the Church, etc.” One or two more: “There are spirits of devils working miracles to go unto the kings of all the earth.” Footnote: “For all the kings’ courts, the Pope has had his ambassadors to hinder the work of the kingdom of Christ”. If you are a king, you didn’t come out too well in these things, either, princes and the like. So these were not what we mean by the normal notes of the Bible. And needless to say, if you were a king, you don’t want notes. So he did not allow any notes in the Bible.

##### B. Historical Context of the Reformation
I’ll add a little parenthesis here that Mary Tudor or “Bloody Mary” as she was called, comes to the throne and begins a period of persecution. Let me just give a little history. It might be nice to just note these dates. Henry VIII died in 1545. At the end of his life he had made England an Anglican or Protestant nation. I wish we could say that he had very good motives, but the big issue was that the Pope would not let him divorce his wives. He probably should have because he killed some of them as a result of that. There became a clash between Papal authority and Henry VIII over moral issues, in which somebody had better be right. He did make a break with the Papacy. Also, since the Bible is the strength of the reformers in the Reformation, he wants a great Bible, so that the people can read it. He dies in 1545 and he is succeeded by Edward VI, who dies in 1553. For eight years Edward VI reigns and he is also strongly Protestant in orientation. When he dies Mary Tudor becomes queen and she wants to undo the Reformation. She wants to make England Catholic once again. She begins persecuting the reformers there, some 300 of them are put to death. Bible translations are burned and destroyed, all but one, the Great Bible. Why didn’t she try to destroy the Great Bible? It was authorized by a king. If kings make mistakes, queens make mistakes. So, leave those things alone. So, that was not touched.

Coverdale flees to Europe. He would have been martyred by Mary Tudor, except that the King of Denmark interceded on his behalf, and thus he escaped that. She marries Phillip II of Spain and in 1558 the people of England chop her head off. They did not want to go Catholic and above all, do you know how England and Spain got along? They were great rivals and 30 years later you have the Spanish Armada, which is not a cruise line, trying to invade England, so that was too much and she is put to death. In 1558 Queen Elizabeth takes the throne and she reigns until 1603, some 48 years, strongly affirming the Reformation and after Mary Tudor’s death, the fate of England as far as religion is concerned, Protestant or Catholic, is settled in the Protestant camp.

#### 7. Geneva Bible
When Coverdale flees to England under the reign of Mary Tudor, he goes to Geneva and there he produces what is known as the Geneva Bible. This was the revision of the Great Bible and it is the first Bible in the English language that has verses, verse divisions. 1560 is the first time we have verses in our English Bible. The Geneva Bible was rather Calvinistic in its emphasis, but let me comment again. Up to 1560, no English Bible has verse divisions in it. If you look at some of Luther’s writings, he doesn’t say, “In Galatians 3:23 we find…”. He says, “Towards the end of Galatians chapter 3 we find…” Chapters are there, but not verses. The first person who begins this is a printer named Robert Stephanus (aka., Robert Estienne) in 1551. He produces a Greek New Testament in which he makes verse divisions. Chapters are there, now he versifies them. I remember a professor of mine, Bruce Metzger, saying that much of his work was done on horseback as he was riding through France and sometimes the horse went up and the pen went down at inopportune places. We are stuck with them. We will never change verses. How would you ever have a new versification and a commentary where the verses are different, etc. It would be absolute chaos.

Even if they are not perfect, it is much easier to try to find a verse in Psalm 119 if it is numbered. So it became very helpful. This is the first one. It was a very popular translation in Queen Elizabeth’s reign. It had over 70 different editions. The people chose this. They chose this over the Greek Bible that was the people’s Bible. Sometimes this is known as the “breeches Bible” because in Genesis 3 when the Lord saw that Adam and Eve were naked, they translated, “He made them breeches”, so it has been called “the breeches Bible”.

#### 8. The Bishop’s Bible
The bishops in England were not happy with the Geneva Bible. First of all, it was too Calvinistic. This is Geneva, where Calvin was located. By osmosis, it would be Calvinistic there. They knew that there had to be some sort of a new translation because they hoped that instead of having two Bibles, the one in the church, the Great Bible, and the one of the people, the Geneva Bible, they could produce one that would be a compromise that everyone would accept. So they produced the Bishop’s Bible, named because most of the people were either already bishops when they were in the translation process, or later became them.

#### 9. The King James Version
The greatest and most famous translation of the Bible in the English-speaking world that ever was or ever will be takes place beginning in 1604. The new king, King James I orders that a new translation be made, based on the Greek and Hebrew manuscripts, that would take the place of all of the Bibles up to that time. ### I. Introduction to Various Issues in Hermeneutics

#### A. Definition of Hermeneutics
Words that were perfectly good in translation in the past cannot be used today because the language is changing. This process is happening more quickly now than before, meaning no translation can ever be the final one. Even if we had the original Greek and Hebrew texts and translated them into perfect English for the year 2002, by 2050 that language would need to change. We are communicating with people in 2050, and we must convert the way we word the infallible word of God to fit their world, rather than forcing them to convert their thinking back to 2002.

#### B. Importance of Hermeneutics in Biblical Interpretation
When we speak of the Bible being without error, we mean that what the Biblical authors intended by their words is inerrant. While the English translation itself is not inerrant, it is without error to the extent that it faithfully reproduces the original meaning. We are concerned with even the smallest poor translations because the Bible is precious to us; we will never be content with anything less than perfection, even though we know we cannot achieve it in this life.

### II. Historical Issues in Hermeneutics

#### A. Pre-modern Approaches to Interpretation
One must wrestle with the basic philosophy of translation: word-for-word versus thought-for-thought. Word-for-word translations, such as the King James, American Standard, and New American Standard, seek the nearest English equivalent. In contrast, thought-for-thought translations, like the New International Version (NIV) or the Revised English Bible, focus on how best to express the author's meaning.

#### B. Modern Approaches to Interpretation
Modern translation challenges often involve gendered language. For example, the Greek word "adelphoi" (brothers) refers to the whole church, leading some to translate it as "brothers and sisters." Similarly, singular pronouns like "him" in Mark 8:34 present difficulties because English lacks a gender-neutral singular pronoun. If the goal is to translate what the author meant rather than simply the words, we must decide whether to reveal corporate terminology in the text or maintain the original pronoun structure.

### III. Literary Issues in Hermeneutics

#### A. The Role of Genre in Interpretation
Different translations serve different purposes. For scholarly comparison of the Gospels, the RSV is excellent for its word-for-word accuracy. For general reading, the NIV or the Revised English Bible may be preferred, while the New Living Bible is often recommended for children. The choice of translation depends entirely on the intended use.

#### B. The Role of Context in Interpretation
When the New King James Version was released, its editors noted they were not trying to make a good translation out of a bad one, but an even better one from good translations. However, some translations, like the New RSV, have faced criticism for allowing external pressures—such as feminist concerns—to potentially obscure the author's original intent, such as when Luke’s intentional pairing of male and female imagery is altered to avoid gendered language.

### IV. Theological Issues in Hermeneutics

#### A. The Role of Systematic Theology in Interpretation
I am generally uncomfortable with translations done by a single individual rather than a committee, as a committee provides more reliability and control. Furthermore, I am uneasy about footnotes and study Bibles. People often attribute an undeserved level of authority to footnotes, treating them with the same awe as the Biblical text itself, which can lead to confusion when a reader's personal interpretation conflicts with the note.

#### B. The Role of Biblical Theology in Interpretation

### I. Introduction

#### A. Definition of hermeneutics
The meaning of a text is that pattern of meaning which the author consciously willed to convey by the words or shareable symbols he used. It is essential to include the word "consciously" to distinguish the author's willed intent from meanings that some might attribute to the subconscious, of which the author was totally oblivious. All three entities—the author, the text (shareable symbols), and the reader—are present in this process. While an author may not be aware of every implication within a pattern, those implications are still consciously willed by the author.

#### B. Purpose of hermeneutics
The purpose is to identify the paradigm or pattern of meaning the author intended. While "pattern of meaning" is the preferred term, it functions as the framework the author wills to convey.

### II. Understanding Words and Phrases

#### A. Word Studies
Word studies involve examining the specific terms used by the author. For example, in 1 Corinthians 16:20, Paul commands a "holy kiss." While the specific physical act is cultural, the underlying principle—the warm, loving concern of Christians toward one another—remains constant across cultures. The expression may vary, but the meaning does not.

#### B. Context
Context is vital for determining meaning. In Mark 7:5-7, Jesus addresses the Pharisees' focus on the tradition of elders regarding hand-washing. By citing Isaiah, Jesus demonstrates that the prophet’s original meaning regarding hypocrites who honor God with their lips while their hearts are far from Him is directly applicable to the Pharisees. Jesus draws out an implication of the original text to address a new situation.

#### C. Grammar and Syntax
Grammar and syntax help define the boundaries of a text's meaning. When examining passages like the "eye for an eye" principle in Exodus 21:23-25, we must look for the underlying paradigm—in this case, that the punishment must fit the crime—rather than limiting the text to literal eyes or teeth.

### III. Understanding Passages and Discourses

#### A. Discourse Analysis
Discourse analysis allows us to see how implications flow from a text. For instance, in Deuteronomy 22:8, the command to build a parapet for a roof is rooted in a concern for the safety of others. This principle extends to modern situations, such as securing a swimming pool or managing a dangerous dog.

#### B. Literary Context
Narrative passages also contain implications. In the story of Jesus calming the storm in Mark, the text is not a manual on boat building or meteorology; its high point is the identity of Jesus. The implication is that the Jesus who can still a storm is big enough to handle the personal struggles and anxieties of the reader.

#### C. Historical and Cultural Context
Historical and cultural factors often dictate the specific application of a text. In the case of the "holy kiss," the cultural expression of warmth varies, but the command to express love remains. Similarly, the Jewish practice of using phylacteries (Deuteronomy 6:6-9) serves as a physical reminder of a deeper principle: that the word of God should always be before us and integrated into the training of our children.

### IV. Understanding Books and Canon

#### A. Canonical Context
Canonical context helps us understand how specific sayings fit into the broader scope of Scripture. When Matthew and Mark record different lists of sins coming from the heart, they are acting as inspired interpreters. They are drawing out implications of Jesus' teaching that are particularly relevant to their specific communities.

#### B. Genre Analysis
Different genres require different approaches to implications. Whether dealing with law, narrative, or prophecy, the author remains in control of the implications. The text does not generate implications on its own; rather, the author’s willed meaning provides the pattern from which legitimate implications flow.

#### C. Theological Context
Theological context allows us to apply texts like Acts 1:8, where the command to be witnesses in Jerusalem, Judea, and to the ends of the earth establishes a pattern for all of God's people. While the general implication is clear, the specific way that calling applies to an individual—such as a daughter serving as a missionary or a son leading a church—is a matter of personal guidance through the Holy Spirit.

### V. Conclusion

#### A. Importance of Vocabulary for Interpretation
Understanding these definitions and the nature of implications is crucial for accurate biblical interpretation. By focusing on what the author consciously willed, we avoid attributing our own subjective ideas to the text.

#### B. Continued Study and Practice
We must continue to study how these principles apply to our lives. While general implications can be discerned through study, specific applications often require the guidance of the Holy Spirit, a topic for further exploration.

<h2 id="lesson-9-vocabulary-for-interpretation-part-2">Lesson 9: Vocabulary for Interpretation (Part 2)</h2>

### I. Introduction
Significance refers to how a reader responds to the meaning of a text. Implications are determined by the author and are a mental discovery, whereas significance is something we are masters of. While meaning and implications belong to the author, the reader is the master of significance, which involves the volitional—the heart and the decision of what to do with the meaning. It is a matter of obedience or disobedience, a "yes" or "no" response.

### II. Types of Vocabulary
#### A. Subject Matter
Subject matter refers to the "stuff" talked about in the text. The Bible is a vast world of subject matter, such as marriage, history, or military tactics. If you study the Bible for anything other than meaning, you are dealing with subject matter. To find the meaning, one must ask, "I have told you this about [subject] because..." rather than just focusing on the information itself. One must also distinguish between description and prescription; the Bible may describe a custom without prescribing it as a rule for us.

#### B. Understanding
Understanding refers to the correct mental grasp of what the author intended. It is purely mental. If two people have a correct understanding of a text, their understandings are identical, even if one is more complete or fragmentary than the other.

#### C. Interpretation
Interpretation is the verbal or written expression of our understanding. While understandings may be correct and identical, interpretations are verbalizations that tend to vary between people. The moment an understanding is expressed, it becomes an interpretation.

### III. Historical-Cultural Vocabulary
#### A. Mental Acts
Mental acts are the experiences a writer goes through when writing. C.S. Lewis, in "Fernseed and Elephants," argues that critics often fail when they attempt to reconstruct the mental history or intentions of an author. If we cannot accurately reconstruct the mental acts of contemporary authors we know, it is impossible to reconstruct the mental acts of Biblical authors from thousands of years ago. We should focus on what the author meant by the words provided rather than trying to guess their internal experiences.

#### B. Norms of Language
The norms of language are the range of meanings allowed by the words or verbal symbols of a text. Dictionaries help identify these possibilities. Because words can have multiple meanings, the context is required to narrow down the specific intended meaning.

#### C. Norms of Utterance
The norms of utterance refer to the specific meaning the author intended. While the norms of language provide the possibilities, the context—such as the rest of the sentence, paragraph, or chapter—helps determine the specific meaning. A concordance is a useful tool for seeing how an author uses a word elsewhere to help clarify the norms of utterance.

### IV. Literary Vocabulary
#### A. Literary Genre
Literary genre is the literary form used by the author and the rules that govern that form. Understanding these rules is essential for correctly approaching different parts of the Bible.

#### B. Context
Context is defined here as the willed meaning an author gives to the literary material surrounding the text. It is the totality of the author's intended meaning within the surrounding structure.

### V. Theological Vocabulary
#### A. Conceptual Framework
It is important to maintain precise definitions for these terms. While the world may use these words differently, we must use our conceptual framework to translate what others are saying into our vocabulary to ensure clarity.

### VI. Conclusion
#### A. Summary of key points
When evaluating the goals of hermeneutics, we must be careful with definitions. Some scholars suggest the goal is to hear the message as the original audience would have heard it. However, we must distinguish between what they *did* understand and what they *should* have understood, as original audiences could misunderstand the author.

#### B. Importance of studying vocabulary for interpretation
The goal of hermeneutics is to enable interpreters to arrive at the meaning the biblical writers intended their readers to understand. We rely on the fact that authors use shareable symbols to communicate. While some definitions focus on the probable intention of the author and the probable understanding of the readers, we will stick to our consistent, defined vocabulary throughout the semester.

### VII. Additional Perspectives on Meaning
#### A. Our goal remains to hear the message of the Bible as the original audiences would [should] have heard it or as the first readers would [should] have understood it.

#### B. We are convinced that the goal of hermeneutics is to enable interpreters to arrive at the meaning of the text that the biblical writers or editors intended their reader to understand. (pg. 97)

#### C. We presuppose the goal of hermeneutics to be the meaning the biblical writers ‘meant’ to communicate at the time of the communication, at least to the extent that those intentions are recoverable in the texts they produced. (pg. 98)

#### D. Though a given passage may be capable of being understood in several ways, our goal is to determine what (of those various possible meanings) the text most likely would have meant to its original readers because that is why people communicate: they intend for what they communicate to be understood as they communicated it. (pg. 133)

<h2 id="lesson-10-role-of-the-holy-spirit-part-1">Lesson 10: Role of the Holy Spirit (Part 1)</h2>

### I. Introduction

#### A. Definition of the role of the Holy Spirit in biblical hermeneutics
For the past two lectures, we dealt with the issue of hermeneutics, specifically the components of the author, the reader, and the text. I have argued that the author determines the meaning, and our task as Biblical exegetes is to find out what the Biblical author, inspired by God, sought to convey through their words. However, we have not yet addressed the role of the Holy Spirit in this process, nor where the Spirit fits into the task of interpreting the text.

#### B. Overview of the lessons on the role of the Holy Spirit
We must examine what various scholars say about the process of interpretation. J. Robertson McQuilken argues that faith is a prerequisite for understanding God’s Word, noting that while an unbeliever might grasp some revealed truth, they cannot fully understand it without the Spirit. Millard Erickson similarly posits that the objective written Scripture, combined with the subjective inner illumination of the Holy Spirit, constitutes authority for the Christian. This dual dimension prevents both sterile, dry truth and ill-advised fervor, leading to the maturity of a "cool head and a warm heart."

### II. Historical Context of the Holy Spirit

#### A. Old Testament references to the Holy Spirit
The Westminster Confession of Faith acknowledges that while the whole counsel of God is set down in Scripture, the inward illumination of the Spirit is necessary for a saving understanding of those revealed truths. This perspective emphasizes that nothing is to be added to Scripture by new revelations, yet the Spirit remains essential for the believer to grasp the inherent meaning of the Bible.

#### B. New Testament references to the Holy Spirit
The New Testament, particularly 1 Corinthians 2:6-16, is central to this discussion. It states that the thoughts of God are known only by the Spirit of God, and that the "natural man" cannot accept these things because they are spiritually appraised. Some modern interpretations, such as those by James DeYoung and Sarah Hurty, suggest that because the Holy Spirit is the ultimate author, we might discover meanings unintended by the human authors. This raises significant concerns regarding how we distinguish the Spirit's meaning from our own subjective readings.

### III. The Work of the Holy Spirit in Biblical Hermeneutics

#### A. Illumination of the meaning of the text
We must distinguish between "understanding" and "significance." The convicting and persuading work of the Holy Spirit relates to the area of significance, where we value truths differently. The Spirit works internally to counter the effects of the Fall, allowing the believer to witness the truth and value the inherent meaning of the Bible.

#### B. Interpretation of the text
Can an unbeliever understand the meaning of Scripture? If we define "understanding" as a correct mental grasp of the author's intended meaning, an unbeliever is capable of this. Just as a student in a secular university could analyze Romans 4 as accurately as a student in a seminary, an unbeliever can grasp the Gospel's content. The difference lies not in the mental grasp, but in the acceptance of its truth.

#### C. Application of the text
When Paul speaks of the "unspiritual man" in 1 Corinthians 2:14, he describes the things of God as "folly" or "foolishness." This is a value judgment, not a cognitive inability to understand. Much like the disciples in Mark 9:32 who "did not understand" Jesus' prediction of His death, they possessed the mental grasp of the words but lacked the acceptance of their significance in God's plan.

### IV. The Person of the Holy Spirit in Biblical Hermeneutics

#### A. The Holy Spirit as a person
The Holy Spirit acts as the great Interpreter. Without Him, our efforts to fully understand the Word are doomed to failure. He provides the subjective dimension that, when paired with the objective written word, leads to spiritual maturity.

#### B. The Holy Spirit as the author of Scripture
While the Holy Spirit is the ultimate author, our hermeneutical task remains focused on the human author's intended meaning. We must be careful not to use the Spirit's role as a license to read our own meanings into the text.

### V. Conclusion

#### A. Summary of the role of the Holy Spirit in biblical hermeneutics
The distinction between head knowledge and heart belief is crucial. One can have a perfect mental grasp of the Gospel—like a professor who knows the text but rejects it—without experiencing the saving significance that the Spirit provides.

#### B. Final thoughts on the significance of the Holy Spirit in biblical interpretation
Ultimately, understanding is a correct mental grasp, while the work of the Spirit moves us toward the acceptance and valuation of that truth. If we had an objective exam on the meaning of a passage, there would be no significant difference between the performance of a believer and an unbeliever; the difference is found in the Spirit-led transformation of the heart.

<h2 id="lesson-12-the-miraculous-in-scripture">Lesson 12: The Miraculous in Scripture</h2>

### I. Three Approaches

#### A. The Supernatural Approach (Traditional)

1. The events really took place.
According to the Supernatural Approach, which is the traditional approach of Christianity through the centuries, the events recorded in the Bible really took place. If you were there, you would have seen the event happen just as it is recorded.

2. The events happened as recorded.
The event happened exactly as the text describes it; it is not a natural occurrence but a divine event.

3. The text proclaims a divine event.
Because the text identifies these as divine events performed by God, any search for a natural cause is irrelevant. God is not limited by human ability to understand or explain these miracles.

4. Since God performed this event, search for a natural cause is irrelevant.
When one looks for natural explanations for miracles, it is basically foolishness. God created the laws of nature, and if He chooses to step in and perform a miracle, He is not violating those laws; He is simply doing what the Sovereign God can do.

5. A closed cause-effect continuum is denied. Openness in history is maintained.
This view denies that history is a closed continuum of cause and effect. Instead, it maintains that history is open to God’s activity.

6. The intention of the text (author's meaning) is maintained.
This approach takes the description of the event seriously and maintains the intention of the biblical author, who clearly meant to convey that a miraculous event occurred.

#### B. The Rationalist Approach

1. The events could not have taken place.
This approach begins with the presupposition that miracles do not happen; therefore, the events as described could not have taken place.

2. The events happened differently than recorded.
While rationalists see a real event behind the text, they believe it must be different from the biblical record because they reject the possibility of the miraculous.

3. The text contains a natural event behind it.
They argue that behind the text is a natural event that can be explained through human logic. For example, they might interpret the feeding of the 5,000 as a social event where people were inspired to share their own food.

4. The natural cause of this event can and should be learned.
The rationalist seeks to strip away the supernatural elements to discover the underlying natural cause of the event.

5. A closed cause-effect continuum affirmed. Openness in history is denied.
This approach maintains a closed continuum of time and space, denying any openness to the supernatural.

6. The intention of the text (authorial meaning) is not maintained.
By rejecting the miraculous nature of the events, the rationalist approach outright rejects what the author intended to teach.

#### C. The Mythical Approach

1. The events could not have taken place.
Like the rationalist approach, this view begins with the presupposition that miracles do not happen.

2. The text proclaims a divine event.
The mythical approach recognizes that the text is indeed proclaiming a supernatural event.

3. The search for natural cause is irrelevant.
Because the text is viewed as a proclamation of a divine event, the mythical approach agrees with the supernaturalist that searching for a natural cause is irrelevant.

4. There is neither a divine nor natural cause, since the text proclaims a myth.
David F. Strauss popularized this view, arguing that since there is no natural cause and the event is not a historical reality, the text must be understood as a myth.

5. A closed cause-effect continuum affirmed. Openness in history denied.
This approach maintains a closed continuum of time and space, denying the possibility of God entering into history.

6. The "deeper" intention of the text (authorial meaning) is maintained.
This approach seeks the "deeper" or subconscious meaning of the author—that which welled up in the author's soul to give birth to the myth—rather than the conscious, willed meaning.

### II. Presuppositions

#### A. Eugene B. Borowitz
Eugene B. Borowitz, a liberal Jewish author, highlights the epistemological divide between traditionalists and liberals. He notes that traditionalists view religion as God-given and revealed, while liberals view it as a product of human wisdom and science. He argues that when these two groups discuss the Bible, they are not having a religious discussion at all, but an epistemological one regarding how one knows what is true.

#### B. Rudolph Bultmann
Rudolph Bultmann argues that one cannot approach the Bible without presuppositions. He asserts that the "Historical Method" requires the presupposition that history is a closed continuum of cause and effect, which cannot be interrupted by supernatural powers. This approach predetermines the outcome of biblical study, creating a fundamental divide between those who are open to the supernatural and those who are closed to it.

In his book, *The Language and Imagery of the Bible*, G.B. Caird discusses the nuances of meaning. He emphasizes that the distinction between sense and referent is indispensable, noting that referent concerns the subject matter, while value relates to significance. Entailment refers to the implications of a word, sense is the simple meaning, and intention relates to the author's purpose, provided they are competent. ### II. The Genre of Proverbs

#### A. Definition: A short, pithy saying that expresses a general truth - exceptions are allowed.

#### B. Examples
1. Proverbs 3:9-10
2. Proverbs 10:3-4
3. Proverbs 13:21
4. Proverbs 15:1
5. Proverbs 22:6
6. Proverbs 22:16
7. Matthew 6:21
8. Matthew 26:52
9. Luke 16:10
10. 1 Corinthians 15:33

Understanding the genre of Proverbs is essential for correct interpretation. Proverbs are short, pithy sayings that express a general truth, but they are not universal laws; they allow for exceptions. For instance, while it is generally true that training a child in the way they should go leads to a positive outcome, it is a mistake to treat this as an absolute guarantee that ignores the reality of human agency or tragic circumstances. Similarly, verses regarding wealth, hard work, and the consequences of sin describe general patterns of life rather than rigid, exceptionless rules.

#### C. Difference between biblical proverbs and other proverbs
The difference between a biblical proverb and non-inspired wisdom is that biblical proverbs are formed by wise people who filter their observations of life through the lens of Scripture and a God-given sense of reality.

#### D. The Problem of Job
1. Job 4:7-9
2. Job 5:15
3. Job 8:3-4, 5-7

The book of Job serves as a vital lesson on the misuse of proverbs. Job’s comforters were not wrong in the proverbs they quoted; they were wrong in their application. They universalized general truths and applied them to Job, who was an exception to the rule. By treating these proverbs as absolute, they caused unnecessary guilt and pain. Instead of using proverbs as a club, they should be understood as general rules for wise living that remain true despite the existence of exceptions.

### I. Judgment Prophecy

#### A. Rule - Judgment prophecy assumes, even if not stated, that if the people repent judgment will not come.

We are entering a touchy area regarding prophetic literature. A helpful starting point is the book of Jonah, which illustrates the rules associated with this genre. In Jonah 3, the prophet is commanded to go to Nineveh and proclaim that the city will be overthrown in forty days. However, when the people repent, God changes His mind and does not bring the calamity. This raises the question of whether Jonah was a false prophet. The answer lies in a rule of prophecy that was understood by the people of that time: when judgment is preached, if the people repent, the judgment will not take place. Even when not explicitly stated, a judgment prophecy assumes an implicit condition: "unless you repent."

#### B. Examples

##### 1. Jonah 3:4, 10
Jonah’s message was a warning rather than an absolute prediction. Because the people of Nineveh turned from their evil ways, God relented. Jonah’s initial reluctance to go to Nineveh stemmed from his knowledge of this rule; he knew that if he preached, the people might repent and be spared, and he desired for them to face the wrath of God.

##### 2. Jeremiah 18:7-10
The book of Jeremiah explicitly confirms this rule. God explains that if He declares a nation will be destroyed, but that nation turns from its evil, He will change His mind about the disaster. Conversely, if He promises to build a nation but it does evil, He will change His mind about the good He intended.

##### 3. Micah 3:12 (cf. Jeremiah 26:16-19)
The prophecy that Zion shall be plowed as a field and Jerusalem become a heap of ruins is another example of a judgment prophecy that did not take place because the people repented.

##### 4. 1 Kings 21:20-29
This passage provides further evidence that repentance can stay the hand of divine judgment.

#### C. Quote from C. B. Caird, pp. 56-57
As G.B. Caird notes, readers often get distracted by the biological details of Jonah’s story rather than the theological difficulty of a prophecy that does not come to pass. Many things were foretold precisely so they might not happen. We must decide if a prophecy is a prediction or a warning; if it is a warning, it carries an unexpressed condition clause. It is characteristic of Semitic style to express ideas absolutely while leaving the listener to fill in the implicit qualifications.

### II. The Language of Prophecy - Cosmic terminology

#### A. Rule - Prophets tend to speak in figurative language, using cosmic terminology.

When we encounter cosmic language in prophecy—such as the sun going dark, the moon not giving light, or stars falling—we must understand the nature of prophetic poetry. While a modern reader might interpret this as the end of the world, the original readers understood this as symbolic, poetic language used to describe the intervention of God in history.

#### B. Examples

##### 1. Isaiah 13:9-11
Isaiah uses cosmic language to describe the judgment of Babylon. While the language sounds apocalyptic, the context clarifies that it refers to the historical destruction of the Babylonian Empire by the Medes.

##### 2. Jeremiah 4:23-28
Jeremiah uses similar cosmic imagery to describe the destruction of Jerusalem. The earth becomes waste and void, and the heavens grow black, illustrating the severity of the judgment.

##### 3. Isaiah 24:23
This passage uses cosmic imagery to describe the Lord’s reign on Mount Zion and in Jerusalem following the oracle concerning Tyre.

##### 4. Joel 2:10, 31; 3:15
These verses continue the pattern of using cosmic terminology to describe divine judgment.

##### 5. Jeremiah 15:9
Here, the cosmic language—the sun going down while it is yet day—is used to describe the shame and disgrace of Jerusalem.

##### 6. Amos 8:9
Amos uses the image of the sun going down at noon to describe the coming judgment.

##### 7. Ezekiel 32:7-8
Ezekiel employs cosmic language to describe the destruction of Pharaoh, King of Egypt, demonstrating that this imagery is a standard poetic device for divine judgment against nations.

##### 8. Habakkuk 3:11
Habakkuk uses cosmic imagery, such as the sun and moon standing still, to describe the power of God in judgment.

Ultimately, the prophetic poet uses this language to declare that the God of Heaven and Earth is acting. These signs are poetic and symbolic, but they point to a literal truth: God is bringing judgment upon the nations.

### I. Sensus Plenior - Matthew 2:14-15

People often raise the question of a *sensus plenior*, asking if there is a deeper meaning regarding a prophecy that the prophet was not aware of, but the New Testament writer was. The second point is even more important. <h2 id="lesson-18-hermeneutics-for-poetry-part-2">Lesson 18: Hermeneutics for Poetry (Part 2)</h2>

### I. Types of Poetry in the Bible

#### A. Synonymous Parallelism
In synonymous parallelism, the same thought is repeated in poetic, rhythmic form. This is helpful because if you do not understand one line, the others help clarify the meaning. Examples include the Lord’s Prayer, where "Hallowed be thy name," "Thy Kingdom come," and "Thy will be done" are synonymous, and Luke 6:27-28, where loving enemies is defined by specific actions like blessing those who curse you.

##### 1. Matthew 7:7-8
Jesus uses this form when He says, "Ask and it shall be given you. Seek and you shall find. Knock and it shall be opened to you." These lines repeat the same essential thought.

##### 2. Luke 6:27-28
This passage provides four lines of synonymous parallelism: "Love your enemies. Do good to those who hate you. Bless those who curse you. Pray for those who abuse you." These lines clarify that loving enemies is about performing good actions.

##### 3. The Lord's Prayer
The three lines "Hallowed be thy name," "Thy Kingdom come," and "Thy will be done" function as synonymous parallelism, with the phrase "On earth as it is in Heaven" applying to all three.

#### B. Antithetic Parallelism
Antithetic parallelism involves repeating the opposite thought, typically across two lines.

##### 1. Matthew 7:17-18
"Every good tree bears good fruit, but the bad tree bears bad fruit. A good tree cannot bear evil fruit, nor can a bad tree bear good fruit."

##### 2. Luke 16:10
"Whoever is faithful in very little, is faithful also in much. And whoever is dishonest in very little, is dishonest also in much."

##### 3. Proverbs
Much of the book of Proverbs, particularly chapters 10 through 15, utilizes antithetical parallelism to contrast wisdom and folly.

#### C. Step/Climactic Parallelism
In step or climactic parallelism, the second line does not repeat the first, but advances it a step further.

##### 1. Matthew 11:40
"Whoever welcomes you, welcomes Me. And whoever welcomes me welcomes the one who sent me."

##### 2. Matthew 5:17
"Think not that I have come to destroy the Law and the Prophets. I have come not to destroy them, but to fulfill them."

#### D. Chiasmic Parallelism
Chiasmic parallelism reverses the order of the thoughts, following an A-B-B-A structure.

##### 1. Matthew 23:12
"Anyone who (A) exalts Himself will be (B) humbled. Anyone who (B) humbles himself, will be (A) exalted."

##### 2. Mark 8:35
"He who (A) saves his life will (B) lose it. He who (B) loses his life for my sake and the Gospels will (A) save it."

### II. Why Use Poetic Language?
Poetry is defined more by rhythm than by rhyme. Jesus used these rhythmic forms because they are easily memorizable, which was essential for an illiterate culture. By placing teachings in poetic form, the community could retain and possess the message before they learned to read or write.

### III. The Genre of Idioms
Idioms are expressions that make no sense if interpreted literally; their meaning is determined by the author's intent rather than the individual words.

#### A. Examples
Common idioms include "God bless you" after a sneeze, "Break a leg" in theater, and "How are you?" which is often a greeting rather than a medical inquiry.

#### B. Biblical Examples
The Bible contains many idioms, such as "Not a man was left," which is an exaggeration for a great victory, or "as numerous as the sand of the sea."

##### 1. Joshua 8:17, Judges 4:16, 2 Kings 10:21 - "No one was left."
These are examples of exaggerated terminology used to describe a total victory.

##### 2. 1 John 3:17 - "Shutteth up his bowels."
This is an idiom for refusing to show pity or help a brother in need.

##### 3. Genesis 22:17ff, Genesis 41:43, Joshua 11:4, Judges 7:12 - "Sands of the seashore."
A common idiomatic expression for a vast, uncountable number.

##### 4. Matthew 17:20, 1 Corinthians 13:2 - "Faith to move mountains"
This is not a literal instruction to move mountains, but an idiom for having great, effective faith.

#### C. How to detect idioms
Idioms are detected by their frequent repetition and the fact that they do not make sense when interpreted literally within the context.

#### D. Specific example - "Love and Hate"
In Hebrew, "hating" someone can be an idiom for loving them less than someone else.

##### 1. Malachi 1:2-3, Romans 9:13
"Jacob have I loved, Esau have I hated." This is not literal hatred but an expression of preference.

##### 2. Explanation from Genesis 29:31
The text notes that Jacob loved Rachel more than Leah, and then refers to Leah as being "hated," showing the two terms are synonymous with preference.

##### 3. Deuteronomy 21:15-17
This passage discusses a man with two wives, one "loved" and one "hated," confirming that the terms refer to the preference of one over the other.

##### 4. Luke 14:26
Jesus’ command to "hate" one's father and mother is an idiom for loving Him more than one's family.

##### 5. Proverbs 13:24
This follows the same idiomatic pattern regarding preference and discipline.

#### E. Meaning is determined by the author
The existence of idioms proves that a text does not bear meaning in and of itself; rather, meaning is found in what the author intends to communicate through those words.

#### F. Learning a new language

### I. Recognizing Exaggeration in the Teachings of Jesus (part 1)

We are going to use another literary form and the form – you know when you teach, you have to always be careful on how you word things. You don’t want to word things in a way that causes difficulty. For instance, many people might have a problem with saying, “Jesus is exaggerating here,” because for many of us, exaggerating has an air of dishonesty, lack of truthfulness. So I would say, Jesus uses overstatement or hyperbole. And I’d separate overstatement and hyperbole. Hyperbole is literally impossible. Overstatement is to exaggerate, but it can be taken literally, so hyperbole is literally impossible. Overstatement is to interpret something literally that is not meant to be interpreted literally. It is exaggerated. They are both forms of exaggeration. But for many people, the use of exaggerated language has negative connotations.

For instance when Mary turns in her books at 53rd Bank, and the bank examiner comes, do they say, “Oh. Mary’s used exaggeration in the books again?” She is using overstatement. No. Here it is dishonest. How do you know the difference between when this form of exaggerated terminology is acceptable as a genre and when its not? I think it is real simple. If both parties know its exaggerated language, its perfectly legitimate. If only one knows, the one speaking, then its dishonest. Then it is deceitful. But if both know the exaggerated nature of the saying, then it becomes a very powerful form of what we call commissive language. There really is no way of expressing love to someone without exaggerated language. If you eliminate exaggerated language, you are in trouble in any courting, romantic relationship. “My dear Joan, the last time I kissed you, the stars began to explode in the heavens and my heart skipped a beat. Can’t wait to see you Friday.” That’s exaggerated terminology. Lets use more scientific terminology. “My dear Joan, the last time I kissed you, it was just like putting my lips on a piece of raw warm leather.” Well. Which is more accurate? Which is the one that is going to get you into trouble? You have to be able to use language like this to express emotions. And lots of Jesus’ terminology and expressions are exaggerated terminology, but they are known and they are shared and they are powerful.

Now through the history of the Church, most people have been able to detect exaggeration on a kind of common sense intuitive way. Most people know it. There have been tragic examples however this has not been understood and people have plucked out an eye or cut off an arm and mutilated themselves because they misunderstood the nature of the saying. What I want to do is to talk first of all about how you can detect exaggerated terminology and then after we have done that, talk about the strength and value of such terminology.

### II. Why use exaggeration?
Now why this use of exaggeration? Well, its powerful language. It is language that commissive language, effective, it reveals emotions and the like. When you come across a saying of Jesus that uses exaggerated terminology, what you need to do is say, “This must be especially important, because He is using exaggerated language. This must be so important to Him that He is evoking language to show it is importance and in many ways it is these things that give us the flavor of the heart of Jesus much more than the non-exaggerated language. You can get a feel of the heartbeat of Jesus when He uses exaggeration. So I would say, rather than simply say, “Well. You just have to make sure you don’t take it literally, you have to interpret meaning as allowing exceptions and so forth,” I would say, “Boy. This must be really important. ### I. Discussion of Various Parables

The lesson begins by reiterating the four main rules for interpreting parables: parables are not allegories and teach one main point, so one should avoid pressing details; one must seek to understand what Jesus meant by the parable; one must seek to understand what the Evangelist meant by the parable; and one must identify the implications and significance for oneself. Dr. Stein emphasizes that if one understands a parable within the setting of Jesus, it often serves as a disturbing challenge to the original audience by drastically changing their perspective. He defines a parable as a brief or extended comparison and notes that he does not provide a formal definition, though he has written on the subject elsewhere.

#### A. Parable of the Hidden Treasure (Matthew 13:44)

Dr. Stein introduces the Parable of the Hidden Treasure as a brief similitude. He illustrates the context of the story with a hypothetical scenario involving a family hiding valuables during a time of war, only for the information to be lost over generations. When a man discovers the treasure by accident, he goes to great lengths to purchase the field. Dr. Stein argues that the character of the man is irrelevant to the point of the parable. The focus is not on the man's ethics, but on the fact that the Kingdom of God is the greatest treasure one can possess, and one should be willing to sacrifice everything to obtain it.

#### B. Parable of Ten Virgins (Matthew 25:1-13)

In discussing the Parable of the Ten Virgins, Dr. Stein highlights a common interpretive error: focusing on the details of the oil rather than the main point. He recounts an anecdote about a preacher who incorrectly interpreted the parable as a lesson on sharing blessings, whereas the point is simply to be ready for the Lord's return. The wise virgins were prepared, and the foolish were not. Dr. Stein notes that while some might attempt to allegorize the 50/50 split of the virgins, the primary takeaway is the necessity of readiness, a theme established throughout the preceding chapters of Matthew.

#### C. Parable of the Dishonest Manager (Luke 16:1-8)

Addressing the Parable of the Dishonest Manager, Dr. Stein acknowledges that many commentators struggle with the master commending a dishonest steward. He warns against creating elaborate, unsupported theories to justify the steward's actions. Instead, he asserts that the master commends the steward not for his integrity, but for his shrewdness in preparing for his impending judgment. The point of the parable is to challenge the audience to be as wise in preparing for their encounter with God as the steward was in preparing for his dismissal. In response to a student's question, Dr. Stein links this to the concept of stewardship, noting that one's use of worldly wealth reflects their preparation for eternal habitations.

#### D. Parable of the Lost Sheep (Compare Luke 15:3-7 to Matthew 18:12-14)

Finally, Dr. Stein compares the Parable of the Lost Sheep in Luke 15 and Matthew 18 to demonstrate how different Evangelists emphasize different implications. In Luke, the sheep is "lost," reflecting Jesus' defense of eating with sinners. In Matthew, the sheep is "straying," and the context focuses on church relationships and the restoration of believers who have drifted away. Dr. Stein explains that Matthew is acting as an ambassador for Jesus, applying the core message to the specific needs of his church community. He concludes that these accounts are not contradictory but represent inspired, complementary ways of conveying the heart of God, and he advises that preachers should focus on the intended application for their specific congregations rather than getting lost in the technicalities of harmonization.

<h2 id="lesson-24-hermeneutics-for-parables-part-4">Lesson 24: Hermeneutics for Parables (Part 4)</h2>

### I. DISCUSSION OF VARIOUS PARABLES

#### A. Laborers in the Vineyard (Matthew 20:1-16)

The parable of the laborers in the vineyard is a difficult text that often leaves readers feeling that the first-hour workers were treated unfairly compared to those hired at the eleventh hour. Historically, scholars like Irenaeus and Origen applied allegorical interpretations to the characters, assigning specific historical or theological eras to each group of workers. Other common interpretations suggest the parable teaches the sovereignty of God or the doctrine of justification by faith. However, these interpretations face logical challenges, such as the fact that the householder is not truly sovereign over the first-hour workers because he is bound by his initial agreement, and the idea that salvation is earned by works for some and grace for others is theologically problematic.

#### B. Sub-rules for interpreting parables:

##### 1. Determine the main characters
When analyzing a parable with multiple characters, one must identify the most important figures. In this parable, the third, sixth, and ninth-hour workers are irrelevant to the payoff, leaving the householder and the first-hour workers as the primary focus.

##### 2. Rule of End Stress
Good storytelling builds toward the final point. By examining the structure of the parable, one can see that the focus is not on the grace shown to the latecomers, but on the grumbling of the first-hour workers. The key to the parable is the householder’s question, "Do you begrudge my generosity?" which mirrors the attitude of the Pharisees and Scribes toward the inclusion of tax collectors and sinners in the Kingdom of God.

##### 3. What gets the most press
The amount of space devoted to a specific interaction indicates its importance. In this account, the majority of the text is dedicated to the conversation between the householder and the first-hour workers, confirming that they are the primary targets of the lesson.

#### C. The Prodigal Son
The parable of the prodigal son is a beautifully told story that must be distinguished between its "picture" (the story itself) and its "reality" (the point being made). Jesus uses this narrative to reflect his understanding of the two great commandments—loving God and loving one's neighbor. The older brother serves as a parallel to the Pharisees and Scribes, who refused to rejoice when sinners repented. Just as with the previous parable, the label one gives the story—whether it is about the prodigal son, the older brother, or the gracious father—determines the interpretation. Allegorical readings, such as those by Tertullian, are unlikely to have been understood by Jesus' original audience and should be avoided. Ultimately, the parable serves as a call for the "older brother" types to set aside their resentment and join in the joy of the Father’s grace.

<h2 id="lesson-25-allegory-in-parables-part-1">Lesson 25: Allegory in Parables (Part 1)</h2>

### I. PARABLES

#### A. Detecting allegory in a parable

When interpreting parables, the general rule is to seek the one main point rather than pressing the details, as parables are not the same as allegories. However, there are specific instances where we must look for meaning in the details themselves. To detect the presence of allegory, we must ask two primary questions: First, would Jesus and His original audience have attributed meaning to these details? Second, would the evangelists’ audience have understood these details as being allegorical? If the historical and cultural context suggests that the hearers would have immediately associated specific symbols with known realities—such as the vineyard in Isaiah 5 representing the nation of Israel—then we are justified in attributing allegorical significance to those details. In such cases, the details are not merely decorative; they are significant and intended to convey specific meaning.

#### B. Examples of allegorical parables

##### 1. The Parable of the Tenants (Mark 12:1-12)

In the Parable of the Tenants, the details are clearly meant to be pressed. The vineyard represents the nation of Israel, the owner represents God, and the servants sent to collect produce represent the prophets. The "beloved Son" represents Jesus, highlighting a unique Christological understanding of His relationship with God that is distinct from the servants who came before. Furthermore, the variations in the accounts of Matthew and Luke—specifically the detail of the son being thrown out of the vineyard before being killed—align with the historical reality of Jesus being crucified outside the city of Jerusalem. This indicates that the evangelists and their audiences understood the parable allegorically, including the destruction of the tenants as a reference to the events of A.D. 70.

##### 2. Parable of the Great Banquet (Luke 14:15-24)

The Parable of the Great Banquet serves as another example of allegory. The Kingdom of God is likened to a Messianic banquet, and the two separate "sending outs" of the servants represent the unfolding of God’s plan. The first group invited—those expected to attend—refuse the invitation, representing the Pharisees and Scribes who rejected Jesus’ message. The subsequent invitation to the poor, the crippled, the blind, and the lame, followed by the command to go out further to the roads and lanes, signifies the inclusion of the outcasts of Israel and eventually the Gentile world. Luke’s audience would have understood this double invitation as an allegorical representation of the expansion of the Kingdom of God to those previously considered outsiders, while those who felt entitled to the banquet find themselves excluded.

<h2 id="lesson-26-allegory-in-parables-part-2">Lesson 26: Allegory in Parables (Part 2)</h2>

### I. Final Interpretive Discussions on Parables

#### A. The Final Judgment (Matthew 25:31-46)

The parable of the sheep and the goats, found in Matthew 25:31-46, describes the Son of Man coming in his glory to gather all nations. He separates the people as a shepherd separates sheep from goats, placing the sheep at his right hand and the goats at his left. The King invites those on his right to inherit the kingdom, citing their acts of service: feeding the hungry, giving drink to the thirsty, welcoming the stranger, clothing the naked, caring for the sick, and visiting the prisoner. This passage utilizes synonymous parallelism, repeating these six acts of love in a rhythmic fashion. The righteous respond by asking when they performed these acts, to which the King replies that whatever they did for the "least of these my brethren," they did for him. Conversely, those on the left are condemned for failing to perform these acts, and the King explains that their failure to serve the least of these was a failure to serve him.

While this parable is often interpreted as a call to general food relief and humanitarian aid, the core of the meaning rests on identifying who the "brethren" are in verse 40. By examining the use of the term "brethren" (adelphos) throughout the Gospel of Matthew, it becomes clear that the term refers to the disciples of Jesus. This is supported by Matthew 23:8, where Jesus calls his disciples "brothers," and Matthew 28:10, where he refers to them similarly. Furthermore, in Matthew 12:46-50, Jesus redefines his family as those who do the will of his Father. This is reinforced by the parallel in Matthew 10:40, which states that whoever welcomes a disciple welcomes Jesus. Therefore, the parable teaches that the way one treats the itinerant messengers and followers of Jesus reveals their true attitude toward the Lord himself. The story of the Philippian jailer in Acts 16 serves as a practical example: his radical change in behavior toward Paul after his conversion demonstrates that his heart toward the disciple reflected his heart toward the Christ they represented.

#### B. The Rich Man and Lazarus (Luke 16:19-31)

The parable of the rich man and Lazarus in Luke 16:19-31 is often debated regarding whether it is a historical account or a parable. Some argue it is a real story because it is the only instance where a character is given a name. However, Luke consistently introduces his parables using the Greek word *tis* ("a certain man"), and this passage follows that same pattern. Furthermore, the narrative employs elements that are characteristic of parables rather than historical accounts, such as the dialogue between Hades and Heaven. 

The parable serves to illustrate that if one does not listen to the teachings of Moses and the prophets, they will not be convinced even if someone rises from the dead. This is a poignant observation, as even when Jesus himself rose from the dead, many still refused to believe. The lesson concludes that if a person's heart is not right, they will not be persuaded by miracles; rather, the Scriptures provide sufficient evidence for those whose hearts are prepared to receive the truth.

<h2 id="lesson-28-hermeneutics-for-historical-narrative-part-2">Lesson 28: Hermeneutics for Historical Narrative (Part 2)</h2>

### I. Interpretive Methods

#### A. Supernaturalists
The supernaturalist believes that the event described was true. They talk about the miracle itself, what took place, and what we can learn from the miracle regarding Jesus’ supernatural power. However, they often give their own interpretation to the event, sometimes rejecting the author’s intended meaning by focusing solely on the event itself rather than the author’s purpose.

#### B. Non-supernaturalists (naturalists)

##### 1. Rationalists
Rationalists seek to reconstruct what actually took place by stripping away the miraculous elements. For example, they might interpret the feeding of the 5,000 as a story about people being moved by the example of a young boy sharing his food, which then inspired others in the crowd to share their own hidden provisions, resulting in everyone having enough to eat.

##### 2. Accommodationists
Accommodationists argue that the Biblical authors knew the miracles did not take place but wrote them to teach great Christian values to an audience that believed in miracles. This approach suggests the authors "accommodated" their message into miracle stories. This view never caught on, however, because it implies the greatest moral teachings in the world came from people who were lying.

##### 3. Mythical approach
The mythical approach suggests that the Evangelists believed these stories were true, but that the stories actually arose from the mindset of a believing community. These myths were born out of deep human urges, desires, and hopes—such as the desire for everlasting life. The goal of this approach is to "de-mythologize" the story to find the underlying existential or religious truth.

### II. CLUES TO INTERPRETING HISTORICAL NARRATIVE

#### A. Context
Any individual account should be interpreted in light of the whole narrative. Just as a single piece of a thousand-piece puzzle is best understood when placed within the context of the other nine hundred and ninety-nine pieces, a story in the Gospels or the book of Joshua is best understood when viewed within the context of the entire book.

#### B. Authorial Comments

##### 1. 1 Kings 15:5
The author intrudes into the story to provide clarity, noting that David did what was right in the sight of the Lord all the days of his life, except in the matter of Uriah the Hittite.

##### 2. 1 Kings 22:43
The author provides an editorial comment regarding King Asa, noting that while he did what was right in the sight of the Lord, the high places were not taken away. These editorial intrusions help the reader understand the author’s specific emphasis.

#### C. Repetition

##### 1. Israel’s cyclical experience of rebellion, retribution, repentance, and restoration
This pattern is repeated throughout the book of Judges. Israel rebels against the Lord, faces retribution, cries out in repentance, and is subsequently restored by a deliverer raised up by God.

##### 2. The absence of leadership (See 17:6, 18:1, 19:1, 21:25)
The repeated phrase, "In those days there was no king in Israel; all the people did what was right in their own eyes," serves as a thematic clue. It suggests that the book of Judges views the time without a king as a period of anarchy, looking forward to the establishment of the Davidic throne.

#### D. Authoritative Speakers
Sometimes an author provides a thematic statement that outlines the purpose of the entire book. For example, Acts 1:8 serves as a paradigm for the entire book of Acts, tracing the spread of the Gospel from Jerusalem, to Judea and Samaria, and finally to the ends of the earth.

#### E. Direct discourse
By marking editorial comments, summaries, or insertions throughout a book, a reader can identify the author's repeated emphases. By reading these editorial comments in isolation, one can discern what the author is trying to teach and then evaluate how individual stories fit into that broader theological framework.

### I. Two Types of Covenants

When discussing the genre of a covenant, we must look at the agreements made between parties. There are essentially two kinds of covenants.

#### A. Parity Covenant
A Parity Covenant is a covenant made between equals. It is similar to agreements made among individuals in a community, such as residential rules regarding property maintenance or the use of a backyard.

#### B. Suzerain Covenant
The second type of agreement is named after the ruler who initiates it. A Suzerain is a king, and a Suzerainty treaty is not made between equals. It is a one-way, gracious covenant dictated by the king to the people. While the people may accept or reject it, there is no negotiation; it is a top-down agreement, much like the relationship between a professor and students regarding grades.

### II. Abrahamic Covenant (Genesis 17:1-14)

The Bible begins with a covenant, specifically in Genesis 17, where God makes a covenant with Abraham. God dictates the terms, and it is a gracious, one-sided, everlasting covenant. God promises to make Abraham the ancestor of a multitude of nations and to be God to him and his offspring. Abraham is instructed to keep the covenant, with circumcision serving as the sign of this relationship. Any male who is not circumcised has broken the covenant.

### III. Covenant Language in Scripture

The essence of the biblical message is the idea of a covenant that God unilaterally makes with His people.

#### A. Exodus
In Exodus 2:23-25, the Israelites cry out in their bondage, and God remembers His covenant with Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob. This remembrance is the catalyst for the events of the Exodus.

#### B. Mark 14
During the Last Supper, Jesus takes the cup and says, "This is my blood of the covenant, which is poured out for many."

#### C. Acts 3
Peter, preaching in the temple, references the covenant God gave to the ancestors, stating that in Abraham's descendants, all families of the earth shall be blessed.

#### D. Corinthians
Paul, when instructing the Corinthians about the Lord’s Supper, reiterates that the cup represents the New Covenant in Christ's blood, to be taken in remembrance of Him.

### IV. Parts of a Covenant

#### A. Mosaic Covenant at Mt Sinai (Exodus 20:2ff)

##### 1. The preamble
The covenant begins with the maker identifying himself. In Exodus 20:2, God says, "I am the Lord your God." The term "LORD" represents the sacred name, Yahweh.

##### 2. The prologue
Following the preamble, there is a historical prologue describing what the covenant maker has graciously done for the people. In this instance, God identifies Himself as the one "who brought you out of the land of Egypt, out of the house of slavery."

##### 3. The stipulations

<h2 id="lesson-34-genre-of-covenant-part-2">Lesson 34: Genre of Covenant (Part 2)</h2>

### I. Parts of a Covenant

#### A. Covenant with Joshua (Joshua 24:1ff)

There is oftentimes reference to various witnesses that are present. In verses 3 and 7 of the covenant in Exodus, there is a witness to what is going on. Sometimes a stone or a memorial is erected so that whenever the people pass by it, they will remember the covenant they agreed to and that God graciously made with them. There are frequently blessings and curses associated with the covenant; while not as readily present in this particular one, there are references elsewhere to a covenant where God graciously blesses those who are faithful for 1,000 generations, but will not acquit those who disobey. Then there is the oath that the people make: “We will obey the covenant. All that the Lord has said, we will do.”

##### 1. Preamble
In Joshua 24:1-2a, we have the preamble: "Then Joshua gathered all the tribes of Israel to Shechem, and summoned the elders, the heads, the judges, and the officers of Israel; and they presented themselves before God. And Joshua said to all the people, 'Thus says the LORD, the God of Israel…'" Here you have the Lord—Yahweh—the God—Elohim—of Israel.

##### 2. Historical prologue (extensive in this case)
From verse 2b through verse 13, there is a very extensive historical prologue dealing with past tenses where God has shown what He has done graciously for the people. God recounts leading Abraham, the exodus from Egypt, the protection from the Egyptians, the victory over the Amorites, and the defeat of Balak. He concludes by saying, "I gave you a land on which you had not labored, and towns that you had not built, and you live in them; you eat the fruit of vineyards and oliveyards that you did not plant." All of this is God’s graciousness, and it comes before the stipulations. God’s grace is not based on the stipulations that follow; they are separate.

##### 3. Stipulations
In verses 14 to 21, we have a description of the stipulations. Verse 14 begins in light of the gracious historical activity: "Now therefore revere the LORD, and serve him in sincerity and in faithfulness; put away the gods that your ancestors served beyond the River and in Egypt, and serve the LORD." When Joshua warns them that they cannot serve the Lord because He is a holy and jealous God, the people respond, "No, we will serve the LORD!"

##### 4. Provision for continuous reading
In verses 26-27, we have the provision for continual reading: "Joshua wrote these words in the book of the law of God; and he took a large stone, and set it up there under the oak in the sanctuary of the LORD. Joshua said to all the people, 'See, this stone shall be a witness against us; for it has heard all the words of the LORD that he spoke to us; therefore it shall be a witness against you, if you deal falsely with your God.'"

##### 5. Witnesses
In verses 22 and 23, Joshua says to the people, "You are witnesses against yourselves that you have chosen the LORD, to serve him." And they said, "We are witnesses." This covenant form is a very important hermeneutical tool. It shows that the commandments in the Old Testament are not legalism, but stipulations to people who have graciously entered into a covenant with the Lord. They are guides for the community; if they disobey, they will essentially be thrown out of that covenant.

#### B. Types of law in the OT

When the Reformation took place, they began to wrestle with the issue of what to do with the laws in the Old Testament.

##### 1. Civil laws
There are civil laws or regulations, such as punishment for crimes or the provision of cities of refuge for those who commit accidental homicide. Deuteronomy 19 and 22 contain examples of these civil laws, such as not moving a neighbor's boundary marker or requiring the evidence of two or three witnesses to sustain a charge.

##### 2. Cultic laws
There are cultic laws or regulations, such as those found in Leviticus chapters two through six regarding various sacrifices. Jesus makes a distinction between these and ethical laws in Mark 7, declaring all foods clean. The New Testament sees these cultic regulations as having come to an end; Christians do not feel obligated to follow a particular diet, as God looks at the heart.

##### 3. Moral laws
The ethical aspects of the Old Testament law, such as the Ten Commandments, are a reflection of God’s character and are still binding. They are not arbitrary matters. While we are not a theocracy and cannot enforce these as state law in a secular situation, the biblical teaching that punishment should fit the crime is a good basis for any society. Ultimately, the ethical laws are summarized by the command to love the Lord your God with all your heart, strength, and mind, and your neighbor as yourself.

<h2 id="lesson-35-genre-of-psalms">Lesson 35: Genre of Psalms</h2>

### I. Structure

The book of Psalms is the largest book in the Bible and consists of five parts, or five books, each ending with a doxology.

#### A. Book 1: Psalms 1-41
The first set of Psalms, Book 1, ends at Psalm 41 with the doxology: “Blessed be the Lord, God of Israel from everlasting to everlasting. Amen and Amen.”

#### B. Book 2: Psalms 42-72
The second book consists of Psalms 42 to 72 and ends with the doxology: “Blessed be the Lord, God of Israel who alone does wondrous things. Blessed be His glorious name forever. May His glory fill the whole earth. Amen and Amen.” It concludes by noting that the prayers of David, son of Jesse, are ended.

#### C. Book 3: Psalms 73-89
Book 3 is a shorter section spanning Psalms 73 through 89, which ends with: “Blessed be the Lord forever! Amen and Amen.”

#### D. Book 4: Psalms 90-106
Book 4 covers Psalms 90 to 106, and verse 48 of Psalm 106 provides the doxology: “Blessed be the Lord, the God of Israel from everlasting to everlasting. And all of the people said, ‘Amen. Praise the Lord!’”

#### E. Book 5: Psalms 107-150
The last section, Book 5, spans Psalms 107 to 150. Psalm 150 serves as the entire concluding doxology, calling for everything that breathes to praise the Lord.

### II. Authorship
Of these Psalms, some 73 are attributed to David, which is about half of the total. Twelve are attributed to Asaph, and 11 to the sons of Korah. Nearly two-thirds of the Psalms are devoted to David, Asaph, or the sons of Korah. While much of the editorial work was done by unknown individuals, this work is considered divinely authorized and inspired, as the placement and organization of the Psalms assist in their interpretation.

### III. Categories of Psalms

#### A. Psalms of Zion
These Psalms, such as 46, 48, 76, 84, 87, and 122, focus on Zion, the city where God chose to set His name. Rejoicing in Zion is an expression of rejoicing in the glory of God, as He identified with the city. While these Psalms were sometimes misused by those who thought the city could never be destroyed, they highlight the special significance of God’s presence.

#### B. Royal Psalms
These Psalms, including 2, 18, 20, 21, 45, 72, 89, 101, 132, and 144, sing about the king of Israel—God’s anointed. They express joy and delight in the leader God set upon the throne. While they were sung for the kings of Israel, they also point by implication to the greatest of David’s successors, the Lord Jesus, whom New Testament writers saw as the ultimate fulfillment of these songs.

#### C. Hymns
Hymns to God include Psalms 19, 24, 29, 47, 95-100, and 104. Psalm 100, for example, calls for the earth to make a joyful noise to the Lord, emphasizing His goodness and enduring steadfast love.

#### D. Wisdom Psalms
Wisdom Psalms, such as 1, 37, 49, 73, 112, 127, 128, and 133, offer practical instruction. Psalm 127, for instance, highlights that labor is in vain without the Lord’s building and guarding, and that children are a heritage from Him.

#### E. Penitential Psalms
These are difficult Psalms—6, 32, 38, 51, 102, and 130—where the writer asks God for forgiveness and expresses deep penitence for sin. Psalm 32 illustrates the relief found in acknowledging sin and receiving God’s forgiveness.

#### F. Imprecatory Psalms
The most difficult of all are the imprecatory Psalms: 35, 58, 69, 83, 109, and 137. These Psalms, such as 137, are not requests for personal vengeance but are cries for God’s justice to be manifested against those who have committed brutal acts. They reflect a desire for righteousness to prevail and for evil to be punished by the supreme Judge of heaven and earth.

#### G. Praise Psalms
There are many praise Psalms, including individual praise Psalms (such as 8, 18, 30, 32-34, 40, 66, 75, 81, 92, 103-104, 106, 108, 111-113, 116, 118, 135, 138, 145-150) and group praise Psalms (such as 65, 67, 107, 114, 117, 124, 136). Psalm 146 is an example of a praise Psalm that uses an *inclusio*, beginning and ending with "Praise the Lord."

#### H. Lament Psalms
Nearly one-third of the Psalms (48 in total) are laments. They provide a place for believers to turn when they are in the "Valley of the Shadow."

##### 1. Address to God
The Psalm begins with a direct address to the Lord.

##### 2. Complaint
This is followed by a description of the problem or the need.

##### 3. Prayer for help or deliverance
The psalmist then makes a specific request for God to intervene.

##### 4. Confession of confidence
The psalmist expresses trust in God’s steadfast love despite the circumstances.

##### 5. Praise
The Psalm concludes with a vow or confession of praise, often prophetic in nature, as the psalmist is confident that God has heard the prayer and will act.

<h2 id="lesson-37-hermeneutics-and-the-canon-of-scripture-part-2">Lesson 37: Hermeneutics and the Canon of Scripture (Part 2)</h2>

### I. Clarification of the Canon of Scripture

#### A. Recognition of our 27 NT Books
With Eusebius, you have the homolegoumena and the Antilegomena making up our New Testament. While some have reservations about the Antilegomena, the majority think it is Scripture. Later, figures like Jerome and Augustine, along with the Church Councils of Hippo and Carthage, recognized our 27 books as canonical. This development shows how the recognition of New Testament books took place in the thought of the church. While this can be disturbing to students, one would not expect these books to have come down as gold tablets that everyone recognized immediately. These were the books of the apostles of Jesus Christ, written in various places and brought together over time, with the majority of the New Testament never being questioned.

#### B. The Protestant - Roman Catholic Debate
A major theological issue exists between Protestantism and Roman Catholicism regarding whether the New Testament is an authoritative collection of books or a collection of authoritative books. The Roman Catholic view suggests the authority comes from the collectors—the Church—who pass their authority onto the books. The Reformers argued that the authority of the books comes directly from God, and the Church’s role is simply to recognize that authority. The Church does not make the books authoritative; it recognizes them as such.

### II. Factors in Recognizing Authoritative Books

#### A. Apostolic Authorship
One factor was apostolic authorship: "Was this book written by an apostle?" The Church’s popular understanding of authorship includes Matthew (an apostle), Mark (the right-hand man of Peter), Luke (the right-hand man of Paul), John, and the Pauline letters. Hebrews was associated with Paul, and other books like 1st and 2nd Peter, 1st, 2nd, and 3rd John, Jude, and Revelation were all associated with apostles, which was very influential on the church.

#### B. Continuous Usage
Another factor is the continuous usage of these books by the church. These books were not "Johnny-come-latelys" that were popular in one region but ignored in others; they were used by the church from the beginning. In contrast, books like the *Shepherd of Hermas* were not used continuously and did not gain lasting acceptance.

#### C. Unity and Agreement
A third factor is the unity and agreement with the rest of the Bible. If the Gospels and Paul’s letters are accepted as the Word of God, the church could not accept books that conflicted with them. These other books had to be in harmony with the established Scripture.

#### D. Superintendence of the Holy Spirit
Finally, there is the superintendence of the Holy Spirit in the life of the church. It is highly likely that God, having sent His Son to die and rise again, would guide history to ensure these records were written down, preserved, and recognized. While this may not be a perfect, infallible canonical development, the superintendence of God’s Spirit provides a strong argument for the New Testament being in harmony with God’s inscripturated Word.

### III. Arrangement of NT Books
The New Testament books are arranged in logical, not chronological, order. The Gospels are grouped together, followed by Acts, which provides the history of the followers of Jesus and keeps the two books by Luke together. Paul’s letters are arranged primarily by size, followed by the "Catholic Epistles," which are addressed to the universal church rather than specific locations. Revelation concludes the canon as it brings us to the end of history.

### IV. Concluding Comments

#### A. Inerrancy
When discussing the Bible as the final authority, the Westminster Confession and the Anglican Articles of Religion clarify that the Apocrypha is not part of the canon and holds no divine authority. The authority of Scripture depends not on the testimony of any church, but wholly upon God. Regarding inerrancy, if something is inspired, it refers to the original autographs. Because we can arrive at a nearly 100% pure text, we can say our present Bible is without error, but ultimately, the standard is the original autographs.

#### B. Inspiration
Regarding whether Paul knew his writings would be Scripture, the real issue is whether the writers believed their work should be treated as Scripture. References like 1 Thessalonians 5:27, 2 Thessalonians 3:14, and 1 Corinthians 14:37 show that Paul expected his letters to be obeyed and read in all churches as commands from the Lord. While we may not have every letter Paul wrote, such as the Laodicean letter or the lost correspondence mentioned in Corinthians, we trust in the providence of God that what was necessary for the church was preserved. Not everything an apostle said was necessarily inspired in the same way as their canonical writings, but when they wrote in their capacity as an apostle, they wrote with divine imprimatur.
