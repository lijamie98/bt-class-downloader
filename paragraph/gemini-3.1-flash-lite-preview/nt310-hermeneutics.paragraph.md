## Lesson 2: Introduction to Hermeneutics

The speaker begins by cautioning that while he does not expect people to analyze a sermon’s hermeneutics while listening, one’s convictions about biblical interpretation are inevitably revealed through teaching. He reflects on his own devotional practice, noting that he introduces the author as inspired by the Holy Spirit, requests divine illumination, and asks critical interpretive questions. He emphasizes the importance of establishing context—both by looking at the Old Testament foundation and by examining how the biblical author structured his own work. He also highlights the use of cross-references, the importance of acknowledging the humanity of biblical figures like John the Baptist, and the value of a "sanctified imagination" when engaging with the text.

The speaker defines hermeneutics as the science and art of biblical interpretation, noting that the term originates from the Greek word for explaining or translating, as seen in Nehemiah 8:7-8. He explains that it is a science because it involves an orderly system of rules, yet it is an art because it requires skill, practice, and a "deft touch," acknowledging that even well-meaning Christians may disagree on interpretations. He stresses that hermeneutics serves as the foundation for doctrine, and he references 2 Timothy 2:15 to emphasize the necessity of correctly teaching the word of truth to avoid the frightening prospect of teaching it incorrectly.

To illustrate the benefits of studying Scripture, the speaker points to various passages, including 2 Timothy 3:16-17, Joshua 1:8, and Psalm 1:1-3. He explains that biblical meditation is not about emptying the mind, but rather filling it with God’s Word and "muttering over" it to extract every ounce of meaning, similar to working a lemon drop in one's mouth. He notes that Jesus himself used Scripture to combat temptation, demonstrating that the Bible is a vital tool for the believer. He emphasizes that because the Bible is the Word of God, it is useful for teaching, rebuking, and training, ultimately equipping the believer for every good work.

The speaker argues that humanity is entirely dependent upon God for revelation, as there is an unbridgeable gap between the human mind and the mind of God. He references Deuteronomy 29:29 to explain that while some things remain hidden, God has graciously revealed what is necessary for us to follow Him. He asserts that revelation is God’s sovereign self-disclosure, not a matter of human discovery. He warns that God does not owe revelation to anyone, citing Jesus’s rebuke of the cities that witnessed His miracles, which serves as a sobering reminder of the privilege of receiving God’s Word.

In response to a student’s question regarding the balance between using interpretive tools and relying on the Holy Spirit, the speaker clarifies that he will address the role of the Spirit in depth later. He maintains that hermeneutics is largely intuitive, as it utilizes the same principles of communication we use in everyday life. He concludes that most interpretive errors occur when people treat the Bible differently than they treat other forms of communication—by taking verses out of context, ignoring the setting, or misinterpreting figures of speech—and he encourages students to be intentional and consistent in how they handle the Word of God.


## Lesson 3: Description of Revelation

I want to talk for a little bit now about what revelation is. We’ve looked at the need for it. I said God doesn’t owe revelation to anyone, but God graciously and generously gives revelation of himself, of his plans, tells us who we are. He’s able to do that as our Creator. But how does he do that? And the Scriptures describe two different categories of revelation. They don’t give them these names, so what I’m about to tell you is something that we lay on top of the Bible, but we do that to help us make some sense whenever we talk about revelation.

One of the categories of revelation is what we’ll call general revelation. And general revelation is general in scope and general in substance. General in scope in that it goes out to a broad audience. It goes out to virtually everyone; as long as their faculties are working correctly, they have access to this revelation. They don’t just have access to it, but they have received this revelation. And the revelation they get is very general in substance. It’s big truths about God. The most obvious manner of general revelation is through creation. Listen to Psalm 19:1-3: “The heavens declare the glory of God, and the expanse proclaims the work of his hands. Day after day they pour out speech; night after night they communicate knowledge. There is no speech; there are no words; their voice is not heard.” So, creation testifies to the glory of God. We learn that God is Creator and that he is big and awesome, intelligent, brilliant. All these things. These are truths, and these truths are known. Romans 1:19-21 tells us this. Listen to what Paul writes to the Romans. He says, “Since what can be known about God is evident among them,” among the people, the Gentiles, “because God has shown it to them.” (Notice the language of ‘known,’ it was known.) “For his invisible attributes, that is, his eternal power and divine nature, have been clearly seen since the creation of the world, being understood through what he has made. As a result, people are without excuse. For though they knew God, they did not glorify him as God or show gratitude. Instead, their thinking became worthless, and their senseless hearts were darkened.” There is true knowledge of God for which we will be held accountable.

There are other pathways of general revelation that we find described in the Bible. Our conscience is one where the work of the law is written on our hearts. The providence of God as he directs affairs of humans in history; we learn things about who God is, about justice and such. Romans 1 at the end tells us that the people know the law of God to the point where they know that the evil things they do are deserving of death. And so, they know God to be judge. They know God to be holy. They know God to be law giver. But then that judgment doesn’t come. And Romans 2 tells us that that’s supposed to lead people to repentance, that God is merciful, apparently. That’s a general revelation, because the judgment that we all know we deserve, it doesn’t actually come. But what do people do with these truths of general revelation? Paul describes in Romans that we suppress these truths in unrighteousness and we will be held accountable for that.

Now, let me give a little shout out to general revelation, because I hear some things now and then to where I think it gets a bit of a bum rap. We often hear kind of what I just described, that general revelation is sufficient to condemn, but it’s not sufficient to save. I just said that we’re accountable for this general revelation, that we suppress it in unrighteousness, that’s a moral act for which we are guilty, but the truths of salvation are not found in general revelation. So, it is the case, I suppose, that it’s sufficient to condemn, but not to save. However, I think in asking the question, we’re placing a burden upon general revelation that it was never meant to bear. General revelation and special revelation were always supposed to go together. And special revelation as we’ll find out, is particular truths about God, including the plan of salvation. It’s because of human sin that general revelation is separated from these more particular truths that we’ll call special revelation here in a moment. That’s our fault; it’s not the fault of general revelation. General revelation was never meant to bear a salvific burden. And so general revelation is a generous revelation of who God is, and we should be grateful for it.

God did not grant general revelation in some divine gotcha mode where he’s going to say, “Wow, I nailed you with general revelation, you’re responsible for it. I haven’t given you a path to salvation, so now I can just condemn you.” That’s not the heart of God at all. God is holy and God is just, but he didn’t give general revelation so he can ‘get us’ in the end. He gave us general revelation, as the Book of Acts 17 tells us, so that we might reach out to him. General revelation is an invitation to know God.

Now, I’ve spoken about special revelation. So, what exactly is it? Well, if general revelation is general in scope and general in substance, then special revelation is special in scope and special in substance. I don’t really like that terminology, even though I just used it. I used it because that’s what everyone uses. But I think ‘particular’ would be a better word. Special revelation, in contrast with general, makes us think that there’s some revelation that special and the rest of it’s kind of mundane. It’s just general. That’s not the case. ## Lesson 4: Special Revelation

When considering the implications of the Bible being divine, the first point is that it is authoritative. Because it is the word of God, it demands a different level of engagement than other literature; one reads it expecting to hear from the sovereign Lord. Secondly, this divinity raises the bar for interpretation. While one might not be overly concerned with misinterpreting a secular play, the divine nature of the Bible creates a desire to understand exactly what God means by what He says.

Another implication of divine inspiration is the expectation of unity throughout the entire Bible. Spanning 1,500 years, multiple authors, various cultures, and three languages, the Scriptures would likely be filled with contradictions if not for the Holy Spirit. Because there is one divine mind behind the text, we can expect a consistent, unified story from Genesis to Revelation. Furthermore, the doctrine of inerrancy flows from the doctrine of inspiration. While one cannot prove a negative, the assumption of inerrancy is necessary because every word carries the divine stamp of authority; it is difficult to reconcile the idea of inspiration with the belief that the Bible is full of errors.

Beyond the divine aspect, there is the human aspect, which implies that historical and cultural context is vital. If the Bible had simply been dropped from the sky like a data dump, context would be irrelevant. However, because it was written by specific people to specific audiences in specific times, understanding that context is essential for proper interpretation. The same applies to literary genres. For instance, Hebrew poetry does not rhyme phonetically, but it relies on conceptual parallelism. To understand such texts, one must respect the human literary styles employed by the original authors.

The human element also ensures that the Bible is relatable and applicable. Much of the New Testament is "occasional," written in response to specific situations, such as Paul’s urgent letters to the Galatians or Luke’s orderly account for Theophilus. Because these were real people addressing real concerns, the Scriptures are eminently applicable to our own lives. God could have dictated the Bible or dropped it from the sky, but He chose to use human authors, which makes His word all the more wonderful and accessible.

Every aspect of hermeneutics flows from these divine and human realities. The process of revelation continues through transmission, which involves the writing and copying of the original manuscripts, or "autographs." We do not possess the original manuscripts, perhaps as an act of mercy to prevent idolatry, but we have an abundance of copies. Following transmission comes translation, as the original Hebrew, Aramaic, and Greek must be conveyed into languages we understand.

Finally, the process moves to interpretation and application. Revelation is not complete until it travels from the mind of God to the mind of the reader. Interpretation is crucial, but it is ultimately for the purpose of application. God does not speak merely to be heard; He speaks so that we might be changed. As seen in the writings of Paul, the goal of Scripture is to present people mature in Christ and to foster love from a pure heart. Application is the vital final step, ensuring that God’s revelation truly achieves its purpose of transformation.


## Lesson 5: Where Does the Meaning Lie?

I’ve mentioned a number of times already the importance of meaning. I’ve said things like, “We want to understand what God meant by what he said.” But what is meaning? What is meaning and how do we locate it? How do we figure out what God meant by what he said? Well, Christians have historically been committed to the idea that the meaning of a text lies in the author’s intended meaning. You might hear the words “authorial intent.” And I agree with that entirely.

But by saying that we’re committed to an author’s intended meaning, that implies two very controversial affirmations. First off, it implies that we are what I’m going to call “hermeneutical realists.” Now a realist is someone who believes that there are things that exist outside of my mind. So, for example, as I look out the window right here, I can see trees and I can see power lines. But if I looked away, I might stop thinking about the trees and the power lines. But that doesn’t mean that those cease to exist. Those things have an existence of their own, regardless of whether I’m thinking about them or not. That’s what a realist understands. A non-realist would affirm the opposite, that things really don’t have an existence apart from my thinking about them.

But a realist, a hermeneutical realist, would believe that meaning is prior to and independent of my interpretation and it lies in the text. It was initially in the mind of the author, and then he conveyed that through words, and it’s sitting there waiting for me. Hermeneutical non-realists believe that meaning is not there. Rather, what one finds in the text depends upon the aims and the categories and the perspectives that we bring to it. And the argument of the non-realist is that we bring so much to each text whenever we read it that it’s practically impossible for any two readers to have a completely unified understanding. It’s just not accessible. Furthermore, the author lived a long time ago in a galaxy far, far away, and we just don’t have access to it. There are too many obstacles. And so why even try?

One manifestation of this is what is commonly called reader response. And in reader response, it’s the reader who creates the meaning, not the author. The author may have had a meaning when he initially wrote, but he loses his authority and control over that meaning. And there’s no way that we can go back and verify with him anyway, so what’s the point? No, reading or meaning is a function of the reader. Maybe an acceptable form of this in Christianity is the home Bible study where we often innocently ask, “So, what does this mean to you?” Now, most of the time, that’s actually a good question, because what we want to know is how does this apply to you? What are the implications for you? That’s hopefully what we mean.

But I know you’ve been in Bible studies where someone will say, “Well, that’s not the meaning I got. What this means to me is…” And any time I hear words like that, I want to say, “I really don’t care what it means to you; I want to know what it meant to Paul, or to Moses, or to Peter, or to John. They’re the ones who control meaning not— So, it’s really irrelevant what it means to you.” Now, again, chances are what we probably mean by that is, “How does this apply to you?” At which point, that’s a really important question. So, that’s the first implication or the first assertion here. We are hermeneutical realists. We believe that meaning exists even before I read it, even before I interpret it, and I’m not creating it, I’m discovering it.

But that implies a second assertion that meaning is in fact recoverable, that we can uncover the intended meaning of the author. And that’s difficult. As I said, we’re separated by at least 2000 years, a great geographical distance, vast differences in culture, language. We have this text, it’s been copied, translated, you know, we have access to it. But how can we know the mind of the author? Now that notion is under considerable attack in our current culture. Consider, for example, the debate over constitutional interpretation in the United States. Do we go with the original intent? Conservatives will say, “Yes.” Progressives would wonder why we would want to do such a thing; they would say, “No, no, they lived in a different era.” And they would say that the Constitution is a living, breathing, adaptable document.

With the Scriptures, though, Christianity has historically been maybe more on the conservative side of that, where we would assert that meaning exists, it was conveyed through the words of the author, and that we can discover that through good interpretive method. So, to the question: is there a meaning in this text? We would say, “Yes, there is. It is the author’s meaning. And our job as interpreters is to figure out what the author meant by what he said.” I probably should have said “authors,” because as we’ve already talked about, there’s a human author, there’s also a divine author. Now, that might sound absolutely ridiculous to you. Of course, there are authors. Of course there are. We have books. Authors wrote those books. But that’s not what’s being questioned when people will say authors don’t exist. What they mean by that is an author as the controller of meaning doesn’t exist. The word “authority,” you might recognize, there’s author and authority, there’s some common term going on there. The roots appear to be the same. The etymology of the term authority, or the meaning originally, is a right based on origin. So, we might say that an author has author’s rights because the author created the writing. The author is the one who originates something. And because the author originates meaning, it is the author who has authority, that is author’s rights.

Now, we’ve talked about inspiration that is simultaneously human and divine. As a Christian, we acknowledge God to be the ultimate authority. Now, if the author is the point of origin, then original meaning therefore would be identical with the author’s meaning. All of these words a common root, notions that when bound together reinforce, I would argue, the idea of the Bible’s reliability. God is God. He is the Creator. He’s the controller of truth and meaning, and he has written in his word effectively so that we can understand what he means by what he says.

What’s happened today, though, is that the individual is a free thinker and a free agent, is autonomous, a law unto himself or herself. He relates to humanity, his creation, through words and through his word, the written word. Francis Schaeffer so long ago was right – God is there and he is not silent.

But what does God do in his Word? Have you ever asked that question before? If not, I’m going to try to convince you that’s one of the most important interpretive questions that we could ever ask. If God is who the Bible says that he is and the Scriptures are what they claim to be (the Word of God), then God doesn’t merely just convey information, God does things with his speech. And in so doing, he’s like every other communicative agent. We all do things with our speech. Let’s think for a moment just about what the Bible is. The Bible is identified as the word of God. That is, to disobey or disbelieve anything in the Bible is identical to disobeying or disbelieving God. It says it in the Bible, God said it. But simply asserting that the Bible is the word of God doesn’t tell us how to use it. What does it mean that the Bible is the word of God? I gave to you what’s called the Scripture principle, that the Bible is the word of God. Others have argued…well, they’ve just denied it, the Bible is not the word of God. Others, such as someone like a Karl Barth we would associate with neo-orthodoxy, would argue, “Well, Jesus Christ is really the only word of God.” The Bible testifies to Jesus. And so, one way of saying it would be that the Bible can become the word of God, if God facilitates a divine encounter with Jesus when you read it.

But the Bible is more than that. When we say the Bible is the word of God, what we are saying there is that God speaks through his word. God uses speech to convey his will. God uses speech to do things. And so, I want to walk you through something that philosophers of language call Speech Act Theory. Now, Speech Act Theory is not explicitly in the Bible. You might go to your concordance, you won’t find any of the words that we’re going to use to describe Speech Act Theory there. So, I don’t want to pretend otherwise. And yet I think what you’ll find is that what Speech Act Theory describes is the actual phenomenon of what takes place in speech, including divine speech. It used to be thought that speech was just the conveyance of the state of affairs from one mind to the other. It was almost always descriptive. But in the last 75 years or so, people have been rethinking this and have come up with what I’m going to call Speech Act Theory. And there’s three parts to Speech Act Theory, three parts to every speech act.

The first is the locution. So, a locutionary act is the uttering of words or the writing of symbols or words on a page. You have locuted. Like right now I am locuting. I am saying words that hopefully make some sense to you. I might say “Hello.” Or I’ll say “We’ll take a break in 10 minutes.” Right? That’s a locution. I’ve said something that is sensible. That’s the locution. The second part is the illocution, though. The illocution is what we do when we say something, what we do with our locutions, what are we communicating? So, if I say “Hello,” what am I doing? I’m greeting you. Or I might be trying to get your attention. It’s up to you. You know, I might say, “Hello? Hello? Hello?” The same word can be used to do two different things. One to greet you, one to get your attention. We do lots of things with our words, and we always do things with our words. Sometimes we explain. Other times we greet. We promise, we command. We complain. We lament. We curse. We bless. We do all manner of things when we speak. This is the illocution. What we do.

And then the perlocution is the third part of the Speech Act. This is what we bring about by saying something. And because it’s what we bring about, it’s largely dependent upon the hearer. We persuade, or we surprise, or we inform, or we make feel welcome. These are the results. Now, because it’s what you do when I’m talking, so I locute, and as I locute, I illocute, but the perlocution is up to you. And it might not be exactly what I want. I have an, what we call, an illocutionary intent, what I hope to bring about. But what I actually bring about, that’s largely dependent on you and that’s the perlocution. Sometimes I don’t have very much authority or power to bring about what I want to bring about. Other times I do. That’s referred to as illocutionary force. How much power does the speaker have to bring about his illocutionary intent? That is, how much power does the speaker have to bring about his desired perlocution?

A good way to illustrate this—I have five boys, three of them are still at home. And around bedtime, I might tell my youngest son, Marcos, to go upstairs and tell his older brother, Vicente, to get in bed, brush your teeth and get in bed. And so, I’ve basically deputized Marcus at this point. So, Marcus goes up and he says, “Brush your teeth and get in bed.” That’s the locution and the illocution, what’s he doing? He’s giving a command. Well, Vicente is not all that interested in obeying the commands of his younger brother, Marcos. And so, I’m not a prophet, but I know precisely what will happen. He will probably throw something at Marcos, and tell him to get out of his bedroom. Right? And then I come up and I say, “Brush your teeth and get in bed.” And then hopefully he obeys. Now, the same locution by Marcos and by me, the same illocution, a command…very different perlocution. In one instance, Vicente ignores his brother and stays in the bedroom and doesn’t brush his teeth and doesn’t get in bed. The other instance, I come in because I’m dad, I have more illocutionary force, more power behind my words, more authority, and I’m able to bring about something better. Well, the same thing goes in Scripture, except the author is God. He has a lot of what we’ll call illocutionary force.

So, the most important thing about Speech Act Theory for us when it comes to Bible interpretation, though, is asking this question: what is God doing in this text? What is he doing? Because God does lots of things in Scripture. God tells people how things are. God asserts things. God inquires. God promises. God blesses. God curses. He commands…all these things. And Scripture is a collection, if you will, of human divine speech acts that, through what they say, accomplish several authoritative, cognitive, spiritual, social functions. Ultimately, the goal of God in his speech acts is to change who I am, how I think, and how I behave. So, axiom number three: the Bible is God’s speech to us where God engages us. If it’s true that the Bible is this species, if you will, of divine communicative action, it follows that in using Scripture, we’re not dealing merely with information about God, we’re rather engaging with God himself, with God in communicative action. That means I ought to go to the Bible every time with the anticipation of meeting God, where God is going to speak to me. And he has goals. He does things in his text.

And there’s a lot of biblical support for the idea that God does things through his word. I’ll give you a few verses. Isaiah 55:10-11, “As the rain and snow come down from heaven and do not return there but water the earth, making it bring forth and sprout, giving seed to the sower and bread to the eater, so shall my word be that goes out from my mouth; it shall not return to me empty, but it shall accomplish that which I purpose, and shall succeed in the thing for which I sent it.” Note two things here. God accomplishes things with his speech. And that accomplishment is certain. Bring it down to a human level. John 20:30-31. This is, to me, the most frustrating verse in the entire Bible. John writes, “Now, Jesus did many other signs in the presence of the disciples, which are not written in this book;” It’s like John’s just making fun of us at this point. I know things about Jesus that you don’t. But then he writes, “but these are written so that you may believe that Jesus is the Christ, the Son of God, and that by believing you may have life in his name.” We know what John’s illocutionary intent was. He wanted us to believe. And so, he crafted his gospel so that as we read it, we would be led to believe. That’s what he was doing in the text, trying to foster belief. He’s specific and there’s a strategy and the exact purpose.

So, it seems to me, one of the very first questions of interpretation that we should ask, one of the very first, not the first. The first question would be: what is the context? But the second question that immediately follows is this: what is God doing in this text? What is God doing in this text? And I have a series of verses that I want you to look at now and ask yourself this question: what is God doing in this text? Some of them you might even ask: what is the author doing in this text? What is the human author doing in this text? But look at the list of verses that I’ve given you. 1 John 5:13, Genesis 15:5, Romans 6:12-13, Genesis 1:1, Revelation 20:1-3, Genesis 15:6, Luke 1:53. Look at those and jot down your ideas about what God is doing in these texts.

Okay, let’s take a look at these verses and try to determine what God is doing in the text. 1 John 5:13, “I write these things to you who believe in the name of the Son of God that you may know that you have eternal life.” What’s the author doing? Seems to me that he’s giving assurance, that he has written these things so that those who believe in the name of the Son of God will know that they have eternal life. This is designed to give assurance. Genesis 15:5, “And he,” that is the Lord, “brought him,” Abraham, “outside and said, ‘Look toward heaven and number the stars, if you are able to number them.’ Then he said to him, ‘So shall your offspring be.’” Now what is God doing? God is making a promise there. He’s making a promise in context to Abram. But that’s what he’s doing. Paul wrote Romans 6:12-13. He writes, “Let not sin therefore reign in your mortal bodies, to make you obey their passions. Do not present your members to sin as instruments for unrighteousness, but present yourselves to God as those who have been brought from death to life, and your members to God as instruments for righteousness.” What is Paul doing here? What is the Lord doing here? Well, he’s giving a series of commands, the goal of which, the intent, the illocutionary intent, is to alter our behavior. What is he doing? Commands. Why is he commanding? So that our behavior would change.

Go back to the beginning of the Bible, Genesis 1:1, “In the beginning, God created the heavens and the earth.” What’s Moses doing here? Informing about the past, teaching about the nature of God. This is largely explanatory. It’s teaching us something. As I mentioned before, it establishes the creator/creature, distinction, things of that nature. What is Moses doing? He’s teaching, he’s explaining. Go to the opposite end of the Bible, Revelation 20:1-3. John writes, “Then I saw an angel coming down from heaven, holding in his hand the key to the bottomless pit and a great chain. And he seized the dragon, that ancient serpent, who is the devil and Satan, and bound him for a thousand years and threw him into the pit, and shut it and sealed it over him, so that he might not deceive the nations any longer, until the thousand years were ended. After that, he must be released for a little while.” What is God doing in this text? What is John doing? We might say he’s confusing us. He is trying to make sure that there is division amongst denominations on eschatology and end times, such things. But that would be a little cynical, wouldn’t it? And not true. When we get to the apocalyptic literature, I’m going to say that apocalyptic literature is meant to give hope. It’s meant to inspire perseverance. And so, John here is informing us about the future. He’s telling us what will take place. Why? To give hope. To give hope so that we know that we ought to persevere because we are on the side that wins.

Genesis 15:6, we are told that Abram “believed the Lord and the Lord counted it to him as righteousness.” Now, this might be more explanation of what happened in the past, and it’s certainly at least that so we could say what is God doing in this text. He’s informing us of what had happened in the past, teaching us. But I think he’s doing more here because the New Testament authors will take this passage and they will establish the paradigm of justification by grace through faith from this. Abram was justified, counted as righteousness by the Lord because Abram believed the promise of God. And so, then we are enjoined, whenever we read this, I ought to believe the promises of God as well. What is God doing in this text? He is evoking faith and assurance, as well as telling us about what happened in the past.

When Mary was told that she would have a child and it was explained to her, she responds, almost like in Broadway musical form, by breaking out into song. And she recites these wonderful verses of the Lord, beginning in Luke 1:53, something that we call the Magnificat, because of the first Latin word in the translation, the Latin translation, the Vulgate, of this passage. “And Mary said, ‘My soul magnifies the Lord, and my spirit rejoices in God my Savior, for he has looked on the humble estate of his servant. For behold, from now on all generations will call me blessed; for he who is mighty has done great things for me, and holy is his name. And his mercy is for those who fear him from generation to generation. He has shown strength with his arm; his has scattered the proud in the thoughts of their hearts; he has brought down the mighty from their thrones and exalted those of humble estate; he has filled the hungry with good things and the rich he has sent empty away.’” What is God doing here? What is Mary doing? Well, Mary is praising, but why would Luke include this in his gospel account? Well, I suppose it does demonstrate that Mary knew a lot of Old Testament prophecy, so God had chosen wisely, imagine that, in who he selected to be the mother to his Son. But I don’t think that’s the main purpose to show us just how much Bible Mary knew. I think just as Mary is drawn into praise, we ought to be drawn into praise as well. This is not merely an account of what happened in the past, but, as we read it, it should evoke praise on our part. As you hear or read the story of Mary being informed that she gets to bear the Christ child, our soul should magnify the Lord right along with Mary. We should be drawn into praise. As Mary rehearses the great promises of the Lord that God is keeping now in her son, we too should praise. If you read this passage and you are not drawn into praise, I would argue you have totally misunderstood it and you should go back and reread it, to make sure that you get it right the next time, because God is doing something in that text. Always ask the question: what is God doing in this text?

One example of this is a former pastor of mine at one point was preaching through Hebrews, which is not an easy book in the Bible to preach through. And he got to the mother of all difficult texts, Hebrews 6:1 through however far, one of those great warning passages. Very difficult. What am I supposed to do? And so, he came to me and we talked about it and he bounced his ideas off of me. And there was a chance for us to, you know, think together about such things. And at the end I told him, I said, “You know, I want you to know, though, that this is a warning passage; that’s what God is doing here. And if you preach this passage and I don’t feel warned, then I think you will have failed in preaching that passage, because that’s what God is doing.” And so just thinking in terms of application of Speech Act Theory, if we ask ourselves the question: what is God doing in this text, and we’re teaching that text, shouldn’t we make it our aim to do in the passage what God is doing in the passage, what the author was doing in the passage? You know, we want, anytime we teach or preach, for our words to be attended by the Spirit of God.

But the Spirit of God is omnipotent, independent, sovereign, holy God who does not exist to do our bidding. He is not a genie in the lamp where if I rub it, I can make the Holy Spirit do three things for me. I can’t make him attend my words with power. All I can do is humbly ask. And we always should, every time we teach or preach, we should beseech the Spirit of God to attend our words with power, with his power to accomplish what he wants to accomplish through them. But we need to do one other thing, too. We can study the word and we can interpret it well so that we understand what the Spirit of God is doing. We don’t want to be at cross-purposes with the Spirit. One of the best ways that we can ensure that the Spirit of God will work through our teaching and preaching is if we do with the text, what the Spirit of God did in the text, and is doing in the text. You never want to be at cross-purposes with the Spirit of God. He will win every single time. He may not, depending on his sovereign will, attend your words with power. But I suspect that if you humbly ask, and if you attempt to do in the text what he wants to do in the text, that the Spirit of God will be delighted to use you as you preach and teach his word.

I will end this with a quote from Kevin Vanhooser that I really like. We should practice the hermeneutic of charity and love. We need to give the author the benefit of the doubt because the author is inspired by the Spirit. This is not only our responsibility as we humbly bow before our sovereign God who inspired the text, but it’s our responsibility as loving Christians trying to follow the commands of 1 Corinthians 13, which is, of course, the love chapter. And maybe this love chapter has more to do with hermeneutics than it does with weddings. I’m not sure. Here’s a little play on 1 Corinthians 13 by Kevin Vanhooser. He writes, “Indeed, it may be no exaggeration to say that the ultimate purpose of biblical interpretation is to achieve right relationships with God, with others, and with oneself. After all, Christian truth is in the service of Christian love.” Truth is in the service of love. Then he writes, “If I speak with the tongues of Reformers and of professional theologians, and I have not personal faith in Christ, my theology is nothing but the noisy beating of a snare drum. And if I have analytic powers and the gift of creating coherent conceptual systems of theology, so as to remove liberal objections, and have not personal hope in God, I am nothing. And if I give myself to resolving the debate between supra and infralapsarianism, and to defending inerrancy and to learning the Westminster Catechism, yea, even the larger one, so as to recite it by heart, backwards and forwards, and have not love. I have gained nothing.”

Well, I think that’s super clever, so I like to read it, but I think it’s really true as well. Think about that, Christian truth is in the service of Christian love. Practicing, a hermeneutic of charity, a hermeneutic of submission. What is God doing in this text? What is Paul doing in the text? How can I give, if it is the word of God and God is understood by us to be all good, all loving, merciful, kind, compassionate, holy, just, how do I submit my will to his? How do I submit to what God is doing in the text? And I think that’s the great payoff of Speech Act Theory. It explains what is actually taking place. God does do things in the text and the things that he wants to do in his text, in his word, in us, his children, is wonderful.


## Lesson 6: Thinking Theologically About Meaning

Let’s think about what meaning is, and let’s do that from a theological perspective here. Where does meaning come from? We might say, “Well, the text…you’ve been saying that over and over again. ‘Is there a meaning in this text? What does the text say?’” Of course, the text was written by someone. The text really doesn’t convey meaning autonomously. It’s like, I mean, what is a text? It’s paper and ink. It’s inanimate. How can an inanimate object give meaning? Meaning is really a thought process. Meaning is a construction of thought. The text doesn’t construct thought; it conveys thought. Well, then who reads the text? Maybe it’s the reader that creates meaning. And of course, I’ve already denied that – that was that reader-response idea, the “what it means to me.” And it is not that the reader discerns the meaning, but that the reader gives meaning to the text that is implied in reader-response. And I’ve already said, “No, that’s not the case or it ought not to be the case.” Is the text a window or a mirror? Does it in some way facilitate our own illumination? Does it give us access to another world? And of course, as I said before, meaning is conveyed by an author. It’s the author who is to control meaning.

And so as, again, another Kevin Vanhoozer quote, I’ve quoted him a number of times now, he says hermeneutics is inevitable. Not because the biblical texts are unclear, but because the aims and interests of the interpreter often are. God is a very good communicator. The biblical authors are very, very good writers. Not everything they say is equally clear, but most of the time, most of the time, the fault lies with us, the readers who are trying to interpret this. So where does meaning come from? It comes from the author. And as I’ve said before, the Scriptures have dual authorship. The Scriptures are all inspired by God. They are, every word of the Scriptures, the Word of God. And yet simultaneously, every word is a human word, written by a human author. So, if that’s the case and if hermeneutics is inevitable or necessary because readers and interpreters have different aims and goals, having a theological interest is not arbitrary. It’s actually necessary. We have to understand who God is in order to rightly read the Scriptures. If we’re going to do justice to the nature of the Bible itself, we have to understand it to be what it is: a collection of human speech acts, certainly, but also a unified divine speech act as well. And so, we have to know something about who God is.

And here are some basic presuppositions, it seems to me that we have to have if we’re to understand the Bible correctly. Our doctrine of Scripture is ultimately directly related to our doctrine of God. And here’s one very obvious presupposition we have to have: God is the Lord. We find that in Genesis 1:1 right off the bat. You might notice that we’re not given any sort of apologetic. There’s no like preamble; there’s no prologue that argues for the existence of God. We’re just told right off the bat: God is the creator of everything. And throughout redemptive history, God seeks to identify himself to people as Lord and to teach and demonstrate to them the meaning of that concept. We could say that God is the Lord is the message of the Old Testament, and then it narrows down to Jesus Christ is the Lord is the message of the New Testament. But this Lordship in the Bible is always covenantal. Lordship is covenantal The covenants are those pacts, those agreements, those contracts that God makes. And they are not contracts between equals. God is God and we are not.

In the Old Testament. God as covenant Lord, he selected a certain people from all the nations of the earth to be his own. He rules by law, but the covenant is not merely law. It’s simultaneously a demonstration of grace. God chose Israel by grace because he loved them, not because they were any great shakes. And because all people are sinners, including God’s promised people, his chosen people are sinners, it’s only by grace that there is any covenant blessing at all. The Creator/creature distinction is a covenant relation. It is a Lord/servant relation. We never interact with God as equals. God is Lord of all. And in all of his relations with the world, he speaks and acts as Lord. This God that we encounter in the Bible, if we’re to rightly understand the Bible, we must understand him to be simultaneously transcendent and imminent. If God is the covenant head, he is highly exalted above his people. That is, he is transcendent, the one who is wholly other, W-H-O-L-L-Y, wholly other. But God is not merely the covenant head, He’s the covenant head. He’s deeply involved with us. He gets in the weeds of our life. He is near – that’s what imminent means. So, God is simultaneously God who is high and lifted up, who also stoops down, as the Scriptures say, to meet us where we are at. This combination of transcendence and imminence results in both authority and present. God is imminent. He is present, but even while present, he’s simultaneously transcendent. By virtue of who he is, he has the right to be obeyed. God, if you will, embodies both might and right. He is authoritative. The Scriptures marvel at the transcendence and imminence of God.

If you were to walk down the street from where I teach, it’s a hipster street. And you were to ask people, “Tell me what God is like, tell me what God is like.” If they believe in God at all, which most would say that they believe in God, they would describe God as loving, that’s his job. “God loves me. God loves me.” That might be the truest thing to them that they know: God loves me. But the Bible never assumes the love of God. The Bible never takes it for granted. The Scriptures marvel at the love of God. Rather than, “Oh, God loves me,” it’s more like “God…loves…me.” This awesome God that we serve, the high and lifted up, when the one who is highly exalted is simultaneously the God who stoops down, takes up his children in his arms as a shepherd cares for his sheep. This is the God of the Bible. To rightly understand the Scriptures, we need to have this understanding of who God is. Imagine, if you will, reading the Scriptures convinced that God doesn’t exist. That’s like a nonstarter because the Bible just assumes the existence of God. And then imagine reading the Bible convinced that God hates you and is against you and wants nothing but cursing for you. What sense are you going to be able to make of God’s redemptive story where God takes into himself, at great cost, the means of our salvation? It will make no sense at all. So, these are the kind of presuppositions that are necessary in order to rightly understand the Bible.

Now, these things are spelled out in the Bible as well. But there’s going to be a bit of a hermeneutical spiral, if you will. As we come to know God better, we will understand the Scripture better. And as we understand the Scriptures better, we will come to know God better. And hopefully we are spiraling in on what is true. Another presupposition we have to have is that God is knowable. God is knowable. Again, imagine reading the Scriptures if you’re convinced that we cannot possibly know God, it would be an exercise in futility. It would be like a religious studies thing. Like you know, like for me reading, you know, the Bhagavad Gita or something like that, where I just want to know what bizarre things other people believe. But the Scriptures are not a collection of bizarre things that some people believe. The Scriptures are divine speech acts where God engages us. And so, we need to understand that God is knowable. And that doesn’t mean that my thoughts can be God’s thoughts precisely because God is the Creator and I’m not. So, there’s definitely going to be discontinuity between God’s thoughts and my thoughts as I attempt to know God.

God’s thoughts, for example, are uncreated and eternal; mine are created and limited by time. God’s thoughts decree what is to come. I can’t even get my kids to obey, let alone decree what is to come. God’s thoughts always bring glory and honor to him. Ours often do not and I’ll just generously put it that way. God’s thoughts are original. Ours are derivative. I’m amazed at movies and science fiction and fantasy and horror movies. And we really haven’t made anything up. Everything that we create is just copying something that God has already made. Some sea creature, you know, God is the truly creative one. Ours are largely derivative, by largely, I mean entirely. God doesn’t need anything revealed to him. We’re utterly dependent on revelation from God. God’s thoughts when taken together, constitute perfect wisdom. Our thoughts are often chaotic and dissonant, sometimes contradictory. And no matter how much of himself God reveals to us, there is an essential disproportion between the fullness of his deity and the capacity and intelligence of us as his finite creature. We’ll never know God exhaustively; we’ll never be able to wrap our mental arms around God. But that doesn’t mean we can’t know him truly, for our part.

So, there are continuities between God’s thoughts and ours. Divine and human thought are bound by the same standard of truth after all: God. What’s true for God is true for us. Divine and human thought may be about the same objects. I live in Portland, often called the Rose City, and roses grow really well here, for whatever reason. I’m not much of a botanist or whatever a person who likes flowers would be called. I look at a rose and I think it’s a thing that’s pretty and often red, yellow, or white, and smells good. But I can appreciate it for what it is. Imagine God who knows that rose as Creator. Imagine his ability to rejoice in what was made. But we’re both thinking about the same thing. He just knows it as Creator, I know it as creature. And then both divine and human thoughts can be true as well. If I am thinking God starts after him, then I’m thinking truly. And again, I may not think truly in an exhaustive sense, but I can think of something as being true.

So, I give you a quote from John Frame here, who’s heavily influenced my thinking on this. He writes in his Doctrine of the Knowledge of God, “In Scripture, reality, but God in particular, is known. And our senses, reason and imagination are not barriers to this knowledge. They do not necessarily distort it. Rather, our senses, reason and imagination are themselves revelations of God.” Means that God uses to drive his truth home to us. God is Lord. He will not be shut out of his world. God exercises this Lordship in knowledge, knowledge of him. Because we’re servants our quest for knowledge should reflect the fact that we are servants of the living God. We are to be servants that is, in our knowing. As such, our servanthood should be reflected in our knowledge about God and our knowledge should be subject to God. So, we want to have knowledge about God. Certainly, we want to know right things about him. But the way that we know, knowing is subject to God as well. We believe, for example, in order to understand. 2 Corinthians 10:4-5 Paul writes, “The weapons of our warfare are not of the flesh, but have divine power to destroy strongholds. We destroy arguments and every lofty opinion raised against the knowledge of God, and take every thought captive to obey Christ.” This means that we are not merely to think about the right things. Just make sure you’re always thinking about the right things. Don’t think about bad things, think only about right things. Like a Philippians 4:8 sort of thing, “think about those things that are lovely and admirable and praiseworthy.” But I don’t think that’s what Paul’s getting at here in this passage. He’s not telling us, “Just think about the right things.” He’s saying, “Think rightly about all things. Submit the very act of knowing to the Lordship of Jesus.”

And then there’s the strange things that happen in the Scriptures, especially as you read through the New Testament. What we find out is that knowledge of God actually produces obedience. Peter wrote 2 Peter 1:3, he says, “His,” (God’s) “divine power has granted to us all things that pertain to life and godliness, through the knowledge of him who called us to his own glory and excellence.” Then verse 5, “For this reason make every effort to supplement your faith with virtue, and virtue with knowledge, and knowledge with self-control.” As we grow in our knowledge of God, we are moved towards obedience because God is God. ## Lesson 8: Principles of Hermeneutics

We are finally beginning our study of hermeneutics, the practical application of interpreting the Bible. I want to start with Axiom #5: Hermeneutics is intuitive and not complicated. We are not Gnostics, and I am not here to provide secret keys or hidden mysteries to unlock the text. You have spent your entire life navigating various forms of communication and literary genres, and you have already learned how to interpret meaning through experience. My goal is simply to make you aware of what you already know so that you can apply it intentionally to the Scriptures.

To be a good Bible interpreter, you must first be a faithful Bible reader. Unfortunately, biblical illiteracy is a significant problem in America, even among those who claim to be born-again Christians. Research from the Barna Group reveals startling statistics: many adults cannot name the four Gospels, identify the Ten Commandments, or distinguish between biblical truth and common cultural proverbs like “God helps those who help themselves.” Some even confuse biblical figures with historical or fictional characters. These statistics are tragic because they highlight a lack of familiarity with the very text we claim to follow.

Beyond mere familiarity, we must understand the Bible as a whole to interpret it correctly. While some people avoid the Bible because they find it confusing, most of the text is actually quite straightforward. The truly difficult parts are not the complex passages, but the ones that challenge our pride and sensibilities. To overcome this, I encourage you to make it a practice to read through the entire Bible every year. If you get stuck in the middle of the Old Testament, just keep going. Consistency is critical for everyone, whether you are a pastor or a layperson. Axiom #6 states that faithful Bible interpreters must be fervent Bible readers; the more you read, the more comfortable and capable you will become.

Axiom #7 is the most important rule in hermeneutics: the three most important things are context, context, and context. Just as in real estate, where location is everything, context is the primary determiner of meaning. We all intuitively understand this because we dislike being taken out of context in our daily lives. When someone manipulates our words to mean something we never intended, it is painful. Public figures often use the "out of context" excuse to avoid accountability, but for our purposes, this demonstrates that everyone recognizes context as the authority for meaning. If we do not like being taken out of context, we should be careful not to do the same to the Lord’s words.

There are different levels of context to consider. The broadest level is the entire Bible, as the canon of Scripture tells one unified story. More specifically, we look at three horizons: the textual horizon (the immediate paragraph or sentence), the epochal horizon (where we are in redemptive history and which covenant is in play), and the canonical horizon (how the passage fits into the story of Scripture as a whole). Using clearer passages to interpret less clear ones is a vital part of this process.

Consider the common misuse of Matthew 18:20, "For where two or three are gathered together in my name, I am there among them." Many Christians use this verse to invite Jesus into a prayer meeting, but a study of the context reveals it is actually a passage about church discipline. When you look at the surrounding verses, you see that Jesus is discussing the restoration and reconciliation of a sinning brother. The passage is part of a larger narrative about avoiding sin, the Good Shepherd’s rescue mission, and the necessity of radical forgiveness.

In this context, the "two or three" mentioned are not just any group of people, but witnesses involved in the process of church discipline. This process is Jesus’s prescribed method for keeping the community faithful, beginning with private confrontation and potentially moving toward removal from the church if the person refuses to repent. When Jesus speaks of binding and loosing, he is referring to the authoritative work of the church in carrying out heaven’s will. By promising his presence in verse 20, Jesus is affirming his authority over this difficult process of restoration. While it is certainly true that Jesus is with us when we pray, that is not the specific meaning of this passage. By studying the context, we move from a superficial reading to a deeper, more accurate understanding of the text.


## Lesson 9: Context in Redemptive History

When approaching the Scriptures, it is essential to remember that every passage finds its place within the Bible as a whole. The Bible is not a collection of abstract or clever religious writings, but a story with a definite plot line, setting, characters, and a problem to be fixed. Each part of the canon must be located within the context of redemptive history, which traces the narrative from creation and the fall, through the history of Abraham’s family, the exodus, the establishment of the monarchy, the division and judgment of the kingdoms, and the eventual restoration.

Throughout this redemptive history, the narrative points toward a promised one—a "serpent crusher," a prophet like Moses, a Davidic king, and a suffering servant—who is ultimately revealed as the Messiah, Jesus Christ. Because of the progress of revelation, some commands given to God’s people in earlier stages, such as circumcision or specific food laws, were later abrogated by further development in the redemptive story. Ignoring this flow leads to anachronistic readings, where the Bible is misinterpreted because the time setting of a particular verse is disregarded.

Paying attention to the progress of revelation is crucial, as most parts of the Bible are designed to be read in flow, recognizing that each passage fits between what happened before and what is to come. While some sections like the Psalms or Proverbs may have a less rigid narrative flow, the biblical authors generally employ a deliberate literary strategy. When reading, it is helpful to consider the original context, such as how letters from Paul were intended to be read in their entirety rather than as isolated verses, even if modern study habits sometimes stretch that process over a long period.

To illustrate the importance of reading in flow, one can look at the story of David and Goliath in 1 Samuel 17. To fully grasp this account, one must understand the preceding chapters, which detail the transition from the era of judges to the era of kings. The Israelites had demanded a king to be like other nations, a request that God granted by selecting Saul. Saul’s primary job description was to fight the enemies of Israel, yet when Goliath issued his challenge, Saul failed to act, hiding in his tent.

In contrast, David was anointed as a man after God’s own heart, a status defined not by outward appearance but by his internal condition and the presence of the Spirit of the Lord. When David arrived at the battle line, he demonstrated his kingly worth by caring for the glory of God and the reputation of the living God. Unlike Saul, who relied on armor and human strength, David recognized that the battle belonged to the Lord. By defeating Goliath, David acted as both a warrior king and a shepherd king, shepherding the hearts of the people by teaching them that salvation comes from the Lord.

Ultimately, David serves as a type of the true Messiah. While Saul was an anointed king, he was not the promised one. David, though also an anointed king, was not the final Messiah either, but he was so close that when Jesus Christ arrived, he was recognized by his resemblance to David. Jesus is the one upon whom the Spirit rested without measure, the one who fought and triumphed over the enemies of God, the one who lived entirely for God’s glory, and the one who serves as the Good Shepherd. By reading the story in flow and paying attention to the literary clues provided by the authors, one can see how the entire narrative points toward the fulfillment found in Christ.


## Lesson 10: Biblical Theology

The story of David and Goliath serves as a reminder that the flow of a story must be considered within the context of the Bible as a whole. This necessity highlights the role of biblical theology in hermeneutics. As established in previous axioms, hermeneutics is a theological endeavor; the commitment to finding meaning in texts carries an affirmation of the reality of authors, including the supreme divine author. Out of this divine character flows authority, as God has the right to establish meaning based on who He is and what He has done. With the presupposition that God is knowable and communicative, we approach the act of interpretation, recognizing that the Bible is God’s speech to us. Utilizing speech act theory, we understand that the primary purpose of speech is to do something, and to grasp God’s intended purpose, we must interpret His speech correctly.

It is essential to have the big picture in mind before beginning the process of interpretation, as every verse fits into the entire canon. Attempting to interpret the Bible correctly without understanding the Bible as a whole is folly. Biblical theology provides the means to look at a particular passage and relate it to the total story of the Bible, allowing us to connect any story to the whole message and, consequently, to ourselves. For instance, while some Old Testament statutes regarding cattle or fabric may seem disconnected from modern life, they are part of a larger narrative that includes foundational truths like the prohibition against murder. The task of biblical theology is to examine the development of this story from the Old Testament through the New Testament, uncovering the interrelationships between the two.

Biblical theology differs from systematic theology in that it seeks to understand the Bible on its own terms, allowing the text to establish the agenda rather than imposing external questions upon it. While systematic theology brings our own vocabulary and questions to the text to address life’s ultimate concerns, biblical theology focuses on what is explicitly taught within the canon. Our goal is to achieve a "thick reading" of Scripture, where each passage is interpreted in light of the entire canon, rather than a "thin reading" that treats verses as isolated, stand-alone statements.

Scripture is also a "word act" revelation, where God’s redemptive acts are accompanied by verbal communications of truth. There is a general order in Scripture: God announces what He will do, performs the act, and then explains it. Because the Bible itself is a mighty act of God, our hermeneutic must recognize that revelation is progressive, unfolding from a beginning toward a climax. Axiom #9 states that we cannot interpret the Bible correctly unless we understand the story of Scripture as a whole, as every verse fits into that story in a significant way. As Graeme Goldsworthy notes, biblical theology makes the difference between viewing the Bible as the word of God and viewing it merely as an interesting historical record.

The overarching storyline of the Bible can be summarized as God’s demonstration of His glory through the redemption of His people, that He might dwell with them, and the restoration of His kingdom, that His rule might be acknowledged by all. This is accomplished through the Lord Jesus Christ—the King of that Kingdom—most significantly through His life, death, resurrection, ascension, and return. God’s glory is the primary motivation for His actions, from the creation of the world to the calling of Abraham and the rescue of Israel from Egypt. Throughout history, God has made much of Himself, ensuring that His name is not profaned and that His glory is manifested to the world.

Furthermore, God redeems His people so that He might dwell with them. This covenant promise—"I will be your God, you will be my people"—is woven throughout the Scriptures, from the Garden of Eden to the tabernacle in the wilderness, the temple, and ultimately the incarnation of Jesus. Through the indwelling of the Holy Spirit and the promise of the new heavens and new earth, God’s presence among His people is a central theme. Jesus Christ is the key figure in this narrative, as all prophecy, the Davidic line, and the promises of salvation find their fulfillment in Him. As Jesus taught on the road to Emmaus, all the Scriptures point to Him.

Ultimately, biblical theology is not just about repackaging conceptual content; it is about having one’s heart, mind, and imagination schooled in seeing the world according to the many literary forms and the one canon that constitute the word of God. As we continue our study, we must keep this broad context in mind, as it exercises a constant influence on the meaning of every particular passage we encounter.


## Lesson 11: Historical and Cultural Context

In prior sessions, we established the importance of literary and canonical context when interpreting biblical texts. We must ask how a passage fits into the broader story of the Bible, as the entire canon influences the meaning of any specific verse. However, there is another essential layer to consider: the cultural and historical context. To understand a proverb like Proverbs 22:28, which warns against moving ancient landmarks, we must first grasp what those landmarks were and why they were set. We must determine what the text meant to the original audience before we can discern its significance and application for us today.

One might wonder if it is necessary to be a scholar of ancient history to read the Bible. While a PhD is not required, we must respect the "scandal of historical particularity." This refers to the fact that God chose to speak to specific people, in specific languages, at specific times and places. He did not write the Bible in 21st-century English for a modern audience; he wrote it for people living in places like Corinth or Ephesus. As Christians, we believe history matters because our faith is rooted in real events, such as the bodily resurrection of Jesus, which Paul defended by citing living witnesses. We cannot ignore the original audience and jump straight to our own application; understanding the historical context provides the necessary window into what God was saying to them, which then allows us to connect those truths to our own lives.

Axiom #10 states that for an interpretation to be valid, it must be consistent with the historical and cultural context. If an interpretation would have made no sense to the original audience, it is likely incorrect. Historical cultural context includes any information about the setting that is necessary or helpful for understanding the passage. While it is not always clear which details are most helpful, familiarity with the text makes these judgments easier. Because God is an excellent communicator and the Bible is a cohesive story, adequate historical and cultural context can generally be found within the pages of Scripture itself.

For example, in the letter to the church in Laodicea in Revelation 3, Jesus uses imagery that resonates with the city's actual geography. Laodicea was known for its lukewarm water, while nearby Colosse and Hierapolis were known for cold and hot springs, respectively. By understanding that these cities were real places with distinct characteristics, we can better interpret Jesus’s rebuke of the Laodicean church’s lack of usefulness. Similarly, the parable of the ten virgins in Matthew 25 is best understood through the lens of Ancient Near Eastern wedding customs. Without this context, the focus on the groom and the delay might seem confusing, but when viewed through the lens of the original culture, the eschatological point—to be alert and ready for the Lord’s return—becomes clear.

While extra-biblical information can add color and help adjudicate between rival interpretations, we must be cautious. John Sailhamer warned against allowing historical or archaeological analysis to become a rival to the text itself, suggesting that the Bible should remain the primary source of revelation. We must avoid the error of ignoring the cultural context entirely, which leads to misapplication, as well as the error of overemphasizing it, which can turn a sermon into a history lecture rather than a proclamation of God’s Word. The goal is to move from the original historical situation to the timeless principle, and then to a culturally relevant application for today.


## Lesson 12: Cultural Context

We have been discussing the various contexts of Scripture, specifically moving from historical context to the role of culture. Webster defines culture as the concepts, habits, skills, arts, and institutions of a people at a given time, and we must recognize that everyone—including ourselves—is a product of their culture. To interpret the Bible accurately, we must consider three culturally conditioned parties: the biblical author, the original audience, and the modern reader. Biblical authors were products of their times, which is reflected in their unique writing styles and vocabularies, such as the differences between the cosmopolitan Apostle Paul and the Galilean fisherman Peter. Similarly, the original audience was shaped by their own cultural habits and institutions, which explains why much of the Bible is occasional, written to address specific situations. Finally, we as readers bring our own cultural baggage to the text. While we cannot avoid our cultural lens, we must be cognizant of it, acknowledging that we are not evaluating the Word of God from a neutral, unbiased position.

The extent of this cultural conditioning is universal, and we must be careful not to assume that our own cultural sensibilities are absolute. For example, in the Pacific Northwest, we live in a highly egalitarian culture that views relationships as horizontal, whereas biblical times were defined by vertical chains of authority. When we read texts like 1 Corinthians 11 regarding head coverings, we must realize that while the command was culturally significant to the original audience, it may not carry the same meaning in our context. We often have a tendency to baptize our personal preferences as universal Christian standards, but we must strive to read the Bible as it is, rather than as we are. Furthermore, we should be suspicious of the idea that our current culture has "arrived" or is superior to all those that came before.

Regarding the relationship between revelation and culture, God initiates and progressively unfolds His revelation, accommodating it to the people of that time. This does not mean God "dumbs down" His truth; rather, it means He chooses specific analogies and cultural frameworks to communicate effectively. Jesus, for instance, used parables drawn from the agricultural and social life of his day—such as sowing seeds or losing coins—rather than modern analogies like dropped cell phone calls. This accommodation is necessary because of the Creator/creature distinction; because God’s ways are higher than ours, He must use figures of speech like anthropomorphism (attributing human forms to God), zoomorphism (attributing animal forms to God), and anthropopathism (attributing human emotions to God) to bridge the gap between the divine mind and human understanding. While some argue that God’s emotions are merely figures of speech, I believe they are reflections of the *imago dei*, where God experiences emotions in a way consistent with His unchanging character.

When dealing with these contexts, we must recognize that every statement of Scripture is both absolute and relative. It is absolute because it is the Word of God, but it is relative because it is expressed through the cultural construct of language. We should seek the balance of Scripture rather than viewing apparent contradictions as errors. For instance, when comparing Galatians 3:28, which speaks of equality in Christ, with 1 Timothy 2, which outlines specific roles, we should look for the historical and cultural context that reconciles these passages. A helpful guideline is to examine whether a biblical theology can be developed for a specific command. If we can develop a theology of hospitality or service, we can apply the principle behind commands like "greet one another with a holy kiss" or "wash one another's feet" in culturally relevant ways, even if the literal actions are no longer practiced.

Conversely, some practices, like the Lord’s Supper, remain literal because they are rooted in a deep biblical theology of bread and wine that transcends the immediate cultural moment. Jesus was not merely "making do" with available items; He was drawing on the profound significance of the Passover. In contrast, practices like foot washing or wearing sackcloth were culturally relevant examples used to communicate broader principles of service and repentance. By distinguishing between the cultural particularities and the underlying theological principles, we can better understand how to apply the unchanging Word of God to our own lives. As one student noted, even simple statements like "God is love" require us to define our terms, reminding us that we must always be diligent in our interpretation to ensure we are hearing God’s voice rather than our own cultural echoes.


## Lesson 13: Word Meanings and Word Studies

The next section of this lesson focuses on the word, which serves as the smallest particle of meaning and the building block of the sentence. While words have a range of possible meanings known as a "semantic range," they only possess one specific meaning when used within a particular context. It is essential to interpret words based on their context rather than arbitrarily selecting from a dictionary, as doing so leads to absurd interpretations that we would never apply to everyday conversation.

This issue frequently arises in Bible study when individuals treat the text as a collection of isolated definitions rather than coherent thoughts. While some may find it exciting to explore various dictionary meanings for a single word, this approach often ignores the author's intended meaning. Exceptions to this rule, such as puns or double entendres, are rare and usually intentional; in the vast majority of cases, a word has one singular meaning in its specific context.

Because we are interested in the original meaning of the biblical text, we must look beyond our English translations to the original languages. Etymology, the study of a word's origin and development, is a valuable tool for this purpose. A concordance is often the most accessible and useful resource for conducting these studies. By examining the components of compound words—such as *episkopos* (overseer) or *apostolos* (sent out one)—we can sometimes gain helpful insights, though we must be careful, as the meaning of a word is not always the simple sum of its parts.

Words change in meaning over time, and a single English word may translate several different Greek terms, each with distinct nuances. For example, the English word "know" might represent either *oida* (innate knowledge) or *ginosko* (experiential knowledge), while "life" might translate *zoe*, *psychē*, or *bios*. Relying solely on an English concordance can lead to confusion if one assumes all instances of an English word refer to the same Greek term.

To conduct effective word studies, one must avoid several common fallacies. The "root fallacy" assumes a word's meaning is always tied to its etymology or parts, which is often incorrect. Similarly, "reverse etymology" occurs when we project modern English meanings—such as "dynamite" for *dunamis* or "hilarious" for *hilarós*—back into the original Greek. We must also be wary of "verbal parallelomania," the tendency to force a meaning from one passage onto another simply because the same word is used.

Ultimately, the best indicator of a word's meaning is its context. While tools like *Vine’s Expository Dictionary* can be helpful, they should be used with caution, especially by those who do not know the original languages. We must also recognize that different biblical authors may use the same word in different ways, and we must distinguish between literal and metaphorical usage. By applying the golden rule—treating the biblical authors with the same respect we would show a friend in conversation—we can use word studies as a powerful, responsible tool for understanding the Scriptures.


## Lesson 14: Implications of Genre

We are transitioning into the second half of hermeneutics, often called "special hermeneutics," where we examine interpretive guidelines for the various genres found in Scripture. A genre is simply a kind of literature, and the core idea is that all literary genres come with their own specific rules of interpretation. While this might sound complicated, you likely already practice this intuitively every day. You seamlessly differentiate between a baseball box score, an opinion column, a comic strip, a recipe on a box of Pop-Tarts, and a stop sign. You understand that each of these requires different interpretive rules, and you have been doing this your entire life.

The same principle applies to the literary genres within the Bible. We recognize from experience that the Bible contains different kinds of literature, and these require different rules for interpretation. This is not a mysterious or Gnostic process; it is simply playing by the rules. As Robert Stein noted in his book, *Playing According to the Rules*, if we try to play football by the rules of baseball, we end up with chaos. Just as baseball is defined by its specific equipment and rules, literary genre is an interpretive presentation of experience in an artistic form, governed by specific patterns and designs.

When we talk about the Bible, we must remember there is no single "biblical genre." There are many genres, and a genre is defined as a type of literature with the rules that govern its interpretation. Because the Bible is literature, we must read it as such. If you approach the Psalms—which are poetry—like a theology textbook, you will miss the point. Biblical authors chose specific genres as vehicles to communicate their message, and their assumption is that the reader will understand the rules governing that genre to grasp the intended meaning.

Understanding genre helps us interpret the Bible literally, which means understanding what the author meant. This includes both plain, denotative statements and figurative, connotative language. For example, when Jesus called himself "the door," he was using a figure of speech to communicate a specific truth. He was not literally a door, but in a figurative sense, he was absolutely a door. Identifying the genre allows us to discern when an author is using hyperbole or metaphor to convey a deeper reality.

Consider Jeremiah 20:14-18, where the prophet curses the day of his birth. If we read this as dense prose, we might think Jeremiah is literally wishing harm upon the man who brought news of his birth. However, the formatting of the text signals that we are reading poetry, a genre full of hyperbole and figures of speech. Jeremiah is not actually cursing a specific person; he is using poetic language to express profound despair, disappointment, and frustration regarding his difficult prophetic mission.

Much of our ability to identify these forms is picked up through cultural experience. If you see a letter starting with "Dear Josephine," you know it is a letter. If a story begins with "Once upon a time," you know it is likely a fairy tale. We apply these same cultural cues to the Bible. For instance, Matthew begins his Gospel with a genealogy. While it may seem boring to a modern reader, Matthew uses this specific genre to establish Jesus’ kingly Davidic qualifications and to signal that all people, including those from scandalous or non-Jewish backgrounds, are welcome in Jesus’ family.

Misidentifying a genre can lead to a skewed interpretation. For example, narratives often describe events without necessarily prescribing them; David’s sexual failures are described in Scripture, but they are not prescribed as a model for behavior. Similarly, if we treat every proverb as an absolute, universal promise rather than a general principle, we may face unnecessary disappointment. Furthermore, we must be careful not to use genre identification as an underhanded way to deny a text’s truthfulness or to excuse ourselves from the demands of Scripture, such as when people dismiss the radical commands of the Sermon on the Mount as merely "proverbial." In our next session, we will begin exploring the specifics of these genres, starting with biblical narrative.


## Lesson 15: Interpreting Biblical Narrative (Part 1)

To begin our study of biblical genres, we must consider biblical narrative, which I treat as synonymous with historical narrative. The early church determined the historicity of a text by evaluating its literary genre and identifying specific markers, such as established settings, identified characters, and a clear plot. Unlike parables, which use formal comparisons like “The kingdom of Heaven is like,” historical narratives treat their subjects as real people and events. While the relationship between an author’s intent and historical fact was historically viewed as univocal, the Renaissance and the Protestant Reformation introduced a critical mindset that eventually led to a decline in respect for biblical authority. By the late 1700s, scholars began separating the literal sense of a text from its historical reality, often treating narratives as moral fables or myths to be analyzed through the author’s subconscious rather than as factual accounts.

This academic trend of "getting behind the text" persisted for centuries until scholars like Hans Frie argued that we had lost the intuitive ability to read a story. Because narrative comprises about 60% of the Bible, recovering this skill is essential. Historical narrative is the re-presentation of past events for the purpose of instruction, containing two dimensions: the event itself and the author’s perspective. Biblical authors possessed a clear agenda; they did not merely record facts but provided a divine interpretation of events. This is why we must avoid misreading texts, such as feeling sympathy for Pharaoh in Exodus or misinterpreting the characters in *The Wizard of Oz* by ignoring the author’s explicit framing.

Narratives are inherently selective, as authors cannot record every detail. Moses, for instance, wrote the creation account as a prologue for Israel, focusing on God’s orderly creation to prepare the people for life among pagan nations, rather than answering every scientific question. We have so much narrative in the Bible because stories are relatable, memorable, and cover the breadth of human life. Unlike hagiography, which whitewashes history, biblical narratives present the good with the bad, making them validated by our own experiences. Within this genre, we find various forms, including reports, heroic narratives, prophet stories, and comedies, which are defined by their happy endings and use of irony rather than humor.

Interpreting these narratives requires identifying the setting, characters, and plot. Authors use settings—geographical or chronological—to frame the story, often employing techniques like foreshadowing, parenthetical explanations, or "freezes" to provide necessary context. For example, the interruption of the Joseph narrative by the story of Judah and Tamar serves to highlight the preservation of the messianic line. Characters are equally vital; we learn about them through narrator comments, the speech of other characters, self-description, and their actions. Whether a character is deep or flat, dynamic or static, or serves as a protagonist, antagonist, or foil, their role is intentional.

Finally, when reading these stories, we must identify the plot’s development, which typically moves from an opening to a conflict, a climax, and a resolution. While we may be tempted to identify as the hero of a story, it is more accurate to identify with the disciples or the people of God, who are often portrayed with all their flaws. By recognizing the author’s agenda, the principle of selectivity, and the structural elements of the narrative, we can better engage with the text as it was intended to be read, understanding that the Bible is not just a record of what happened, but a divine interpretation of those events.


## Lesson 16: Interpreting Biblical Narrative (Part 2)

The most important aspect of biblical interpretation is context, which applies to narratives as much as any other genre. Any individual micro-story must be interpreted in light of the whole account, including the specific book in which it is found. For example, Mark chapter five, which details Jesus healing a demoniac, should be read in the context of the entire Gospel of Mark. Mark introduces Jesus as the Son of God and establishes early on that Jesus did not permit demons to speak because they knew his identity. When Jesus allows the demons to speak in chapter five, it is an intentional departure from his established practice, serving to highlight his sovereign authority and demonstrate his superiority over the demonic.

Another strategy for interpreting narrative is to look for thematic verses where the author explicitly states their purpose. John 20 provides a clear example, as the author explains that he selected his material specifically so that readers would believe Jesus is the Christ and find life in his name. Similarly, the Book of Acts follows a trajectory established in chapter one, where Jesus tells his disciples they will be his witnesses in Jerusalem, Judea, Samaria, and to the end of the earth. The rest of the book tracks the fulfillment of this mission. Sometimes these thematic keys appear at the beginning and end of a book, such as in Deuteronomy, where the text reflects on the legacy of Moses and the anticipation of a future prophet.

Repetition is another vital clue for identifying themes within a narrative. The Book of Judges, for instance, follows a recurring cycle: the Israelites do evil, God allows them to be oppressed, they cry out for mercy, and God raises up a deliverer. This pattern repeats throughout the book, serving as a structural framework for the narrative. Likewise, the books of Kings use the recurring phrase regarding whether a king did what was right or evil in the eyes of the Lord to evaluate the leadership of Israel and Judah, illustrating the principle that the spiritual state of the people often followed the example of their king.

Finally, readers should look for authorial comments, which are frequent in the Gospel of John. John often interrupts his narrative to provide parenthetical explanations, such as clarifying that Jesus’s reference to living water in chapter seven actually concerned the Holy Spirit. Because biblical authors were intentional, readers should practice a hermeneutic of charity, assuming that every author had a specific strategy or agenda. By paying attention to the subjects of the stories and the way they are presented, readers can better understand the narrative, keeping in mind the concept of dual authorship, where the Holy Spirit may have intended a deeper meaning than the human author fully grasped.

## Lesson 18: Importance of Prophecy

The next biblical literary genre to examine is prophecy. Having already covered narrative and the law, it is important to understand that about one-fifth of the Bible consists of prophetic material, specifically the major and minor prophets of the Old Testament. While many people equate prophecy solely with the prediction of the future, this is a misconception; prophecy is rarely just about future prediction. However, there are three reasons why future prediction is occasionally important: it vindicates God’s exclusive claims to deity by challenging the false idols of the Old Testament, it reveals future realities that have already invaded the present for believers, and it provides a future perspective that shapes and directs our current course of life.

It is essential to distinguish between prophecy as a literary genre and eschatology. Axiom 17 defines prophecy as direct discourse from God to man, mediated through the prophet. This definition remains consistent across both the Old and New Testaments. While the message is inspired by God, it is mediated through the prophet’s unique personality and thought process. Axiom 18 emphasizes that the prophet was primarily a "forth-teller" rather than a "foreteller." Their main role was to act as a messenger sent from God to call the people back to the covenant. They were not social revolutionaries, but rather conservative voices calling for reformation and a return to the traditions established in the Torah.

The prophets functioned much like prosecuting attorneys, using the blessings and curses outlined in Deuteronomy 28 as the basis for their message. They compared the people's current actions against the covenantal requirements, warning of the consequences of disobedience. Fee and Stewart identify six categories of blessings and ten types of punishment—such as death, disease, and deportation—that form the "grist of the prophetic mill." Most of the prophetic material is not messianic or focused on the distant future; rather, it concerns the immediate future of Israel and serves as a call to repentance.

The key to understanding the prophetic genre, as stated in Axiom 19, is a firm grasp of the covenants—specifically the Abrahamic, Mosaic, and New covenants. Deuteronomy 28 provides the framework for blessing and cursing, while Deuteronomy 30 contains the provision for God to do something new, such as the circumcision of the heart. The prophets recognized that the problem with the Old Covenant was not the law itself, but the people who were party to it. Consequently, God promised a new covenant that would involve changing the hearts of His people, a theme that remains central to the prophetic message.


## Lesson 19: Challenges of Interpreting Prophecy

One of the primary hermeneutical difficulties in studying prophecy is the common misconception that prophecy is exclusively a prediction of the future. When readers approach the Bible with this expectation, they often struggle to interpret texts that function differently. Another significant challenge is historical distance. The prophetic literature is saturated with analogies, geographical references, and cultural nuances that require a solid grasp of the historical context. While some prophets, such as Obadiah, Joel, and Jonah, lack specific chronological markers, others wrote during times of intense political, military, economic, or social upheaval. By consulting books like Kings and Chronicles, readers can better understand the religious and national crises that necessitated the divine message.

A major interpretive issue involves the question of fulfillment, particularly the concept of multiple or double fulfillment. Prophecies like the "abomination that makes desolate" in Daniel appear to have been fulfilled by Antiochus Epiphanes in the second century B.C., yet Jesus and the author of Revelation speak of such events as future occurrences. This suggests that God may give prophecies that are fulfilled multiple times, often with increasing intensity, or that prophets engaged in a "telescoping of time," where they perceived distant events as imminent. Additionally, the concept of conditional prophecy—where a prediction of judgment is implicitly contingent upon the people's response—helps explain why some prophetic warnings do not come to pass, as seen in the story of Jonah and the repentance of Nineveh.

Prophetic literature utilizes a wide variety of forms, including judgment speeches, "woe" oracles, symbolic actions, and disputation speeches. Because there is no "one-size-fits-all" approach, interpreters must identify the specific subgenre of a passage to understand its intent. Furthermore, the question of *sensus plenior*—the idea that a text may hold a deeper meaning intended by God beyond the author's original consciousness—remains a point of debate. While some see this as a way to link Old Testament prophecy to New Testament fulfillment, others caution against using it to bypass the grammatical and historical meaning of the text, preferring to view such connections as legitimate implications rather than hidden, unverifiable meanings.

To interpret prophecy effectively, one must avoid letting contemporary agendas control the text or ignoring the unique role of Old Covenant Israel in redemptive history. It is essential to identify the background, date, and audience of a prophecy before leaping to futuristic interpretations. Readers should balance the historical "forth-telling" with the predictive "foretelling," account for the poetic and figurative nature of the language, and remain self-aware of their own theological biases. Ultimately, while the New Testament provides a guide for understanding how prophecies find their fulfillment in Christ, the interpreter must respect the original context, recognizing that while the heart of God remains consistent, the application of His word for the New Covenant Church differs from its application to the geopolitical entity of ancient Israel.

## Lesson 23: Hermeneutics and Figures of Speech

In this section, we explore the importance of understanding figures of speech, which are prevalent throughout the Bible, particularly in poetry. While learning to interpret them is vital, we already possess a natural, often subconscious, ability to navigate them because we use them constantly in daily life. When determining if a figure of speech is being used, one should initially consider the literal or "wooden" meaning, but then evaluate whether the context allows for or demands a figurative interpretation. If the literal meaning is nonsensical, creepy, or contradicts the context, a figurative construction is likely intended.

Figures of speech are essentially word pictures that use a definite point of comparison to stir the imagination and create expectation. They are not invitations to interpret text however one pleases; rather, they are context-driven tools used by authors to convey specific meaning. To interpret them effectively, one must become familiar with the literature and recognize common types, such as similes, which use "like" or "as" to make formal comparisons, and metaphors, which make implied comparisons without those formal markers.

Other figures of speech include metonymy, where one word is substituted for another based on a close conceptual relationship, and synecdoche, where a part of something stands in for the whole. Hyperbole, or intentional exaggeration, is another common device used to make a point by magnifying reality beyond literal possibility. To identify hyperbole, one should look for statements that are literally impossible, conflict with other biblical teachings or the speaker's own actions, or fail to achieve their desired goal if taken at face value.

Beyond these, there are more nuanced figures like hendiadys, which expresses one idea using two words; personification, which attributes human qualities to inanimate objects or ideas; and litotes, which asserts a point by negating its opposite. While these devices add richness to biblical literature, they require careful study to ensure we are grasping the author's intended meaning rather than imposing our own. Ultimately, recognizing these figures helps us avoid misinterpreting the text, such as taking hyperbolic battle reports or metaphorical commands as literal, thereby allowing us to better understand the true message of the Scriptures.
