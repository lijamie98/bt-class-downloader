## Lesson 1: Early Beginnings (Part 1)

The purpose of this course is to provide students with a brief history of the English Bible, a subject often overlooked in seminary curricula. Understanding how the English Bible came to be is a vital introduction to the study of hermeneutics. This course will cover the goal of interpretation, the influence of presuppositions and genre, and the methods for determining both the original meaning and present significance of ancient texts. The primary goal is to master the technical hermeneutical vocabulary found in R. H. Stein’s *A Basic Guide to Interpreting the Bible*, which will serve as the framework for our discussions.

Students are encouraged to use three recommended texts: *Introduction to Biblical Interpretation* by Klein, Blomberg, and Hubbard; *A Basic Guide to Interpreting the Bible* by Stein; and *The Journey from Text to Translation* by Paul Wegner. Among the many books on hermeneutics, Stein’s work is selected for its user-friendly approach and clarity. By the end of this class, students will possess a new understanding of biblical interpretation, grounded in a consistent, precise vocabulary that ensures clarity in our discussions. We will also explore the history of English translations, the roles of authors and readers, and the specific rules required to interpret the various genres found within the Bible.

The English language itself is a relatively recent development, emerging from the migration of Germanic tribes—the Angles, Saxons, and Jutes—to England in the 5th century. This Anglo-Saxon foundation was later influenced by the French language following the Norman Conquest in 1066. In the earliest periods of English history, the Latin Vulgate was the primary Bible of the Church. Because literacy was rare, the common people learned biblical stories through church art, stained glass, carvings, and the songs of traveling troubadours, who used music to teach scripture to those who could not read.

Early attempts to translate the Bible into Old English began around the 7th century, focusing on essential texts like the Psalms, the Lord’s Prayer, and the Decalogue. John Wycliffe later produced the first complete Bible in the English language in 1382. Because he lacked knowledge of Greek and Hebrew, he translated from the Latin Vulgate. His work sparked significant criticism of the clergy and political leaders, leading authorities to declare reading the English Bible a capital offense by 1414. Despite the persecution of his followers and the posthumous burning of his remains, Wycliffe’s efforts laid the groundwork for future Bible translation.

William Tyndale followed in the era of the printing press, producing the first printed New Testament in 1526. His work was heavily influenced by Lutheran theology, which drew the ire of English authorities. Tyndale was a master of the original languages and possessed a unique talent for translating them into clear, modern English; in fact, 90% of the New Testament in the King James Version is derived from his work. After fleeing to the continent, he was eventually kidnapped and burned at the stake in 1536, famously praying for the King of England’s eyes to be opened.

Following Tyndale’s death, his coworker Miles Coverdale published the first complete printed English Bible. While Coverdale did not know the original languages, he revised Tyndale’s work and utilized the Latin Vulgate and Luther’s German translation to complete the text. Coverdale was also the first to separate the Apocrypha from the Old Testament, placing these books between the Old and New Testaments. This practice continued until the 1700s, when the Apocrypha were eventually omitted from many editions to reduce the cost of printing and to reflect the reformers' view that these books were not part of the canonical Scripture.


## Lesson 2: Early Beginnings (Part 2)

Thomas Matthew, the pseudonym used by John Rogers, produced the Matthew Bible in 1537. Rogers, a coworker of Tyndale, sought to protect his identity following Tyndale’s martyrdom, though he was eventually discovered and executed by Mary Tudor in 1555. This Bible was largely a revision of Tyndale’s work, supplemented by Coverdale’s translations where Tyndale’s were missing. In 1537, both the Matthew and Coverdale Bibles were licensed by Henry VIII, marking a significant moment for English scripture just one year after Tyndale’s death.

The Taverner Bible, published in 1539, continued the trend of revising Tyndale’s foundational work. Translators of this era generally operated under the presupposition that Tyndale’s original work should remain unchanged unless absolutely necessary. This reliance on Tyndale as the central root of English translation persisted throughout the period, as subsequent translators consistently returned to his work as the primary authority.

The Great Bible, named for its massive physical size, was the first version authorized by Henry VIII. Published in 1539 with the support of Thomas Cromwell and Coverdale, it was intended for use in pulpits rather than for personal study. To ensure its authorization, the editors removed all footnotes, which had become controversial due to their often inflammatory, anti-papal commentary found in other versions. The Great Bible remained the only translation spared during the intense persecution under Mary Tudor, who sought to restore Catholicism to England.

Following the death of Henry VIII and the brief reign of Edward VI, Mary Tudor’s ascension led to the persecution of reformers and the destruction of many Bibles. During this time, Coverdale fled to Geneva, where he produced the Geneva Bible in 1560. This version was the first English Bible to include verse divisions, a system popularized by Robert Stephanus in 1551. The Geneva Bible became highly popular during the reign of Queen Elizabeth I, who solidified England as a Protestant nation after Mary Tudor’s death in 1558.

The Bishops’ Bible was created as a compromise to replace both the Great Bible and the Geneva Bible, which the clergy found too Calvinistic. However, it was eventually superseded by the King James Version, commissioned in 1604. This version, produced by forty-seven scholars, became the most famous English translation, with approximately 90% of its New Testament text derived from Tyndale. Aside from a 1769 update to modernize spelling, the King James Version remained the standard for centuries.

Roman Catholics, seeking an alternative to Protestant translations, produced the Douay-Rheims Version between 1609 and 1610. Unlike the Protestant versions based on Greek and Hebrew, this translation was based on the Latin Vulgate, as mandated by the Council of Trent. It served as the authorized Catholic translation until the 20th century. Despite the variety of versions, nearly all English translations trace their lineage back to the foundational work of William Tyndale.

In the modern era, Bible translation has become a frequent process of revision. Since the English Revised Version of 1881, numerous translations—such as the RSV, NIV, and NASB—have been published and updated regularly. While some argue that frequent revisions prevent the trauma of sudden, massive changes, others question the necessity of producing so many new versions, noting that the translation industry has become a significant commercial enterprise while many languages still lack any portion of the Bible.


## Lesson 3: Hermeneutical Issues (Part 1)

The philosophy of Bible translation begins with the axiom that there is no such thing as an exact equivalence of meaning between words in different languages. Because languages are deeply rooted in culture, and no two cultures are identical, translators face significant challenges. For example, the word "spirit" in English carries a range of meanings—from the Holy Spirit to ghosts or alcoholic beverages—that do not map perfectly onto the German "geist" or the Greek "pneuma." This realization highlights that while words may overlap, identical synonyms across languages are rare, making the act of translation a complex problem of cultural mediation.

A practical example of this difficulty is found in the German school system. A "Gymnasium" in Germany is not equivalent to an American "gym" or "junior high," as it is a specific academic track for students preparing for university. Because there is no direct English equivalent, a translator must either use the original term and provide a lengthy explanatory footnote or attempt a clumsy approximation. This same cultural gap appears in the Bible, such as in the account of Joseph and Mary in Matthew 1. Their relationship was a legally binding engagement that could only be dissolved by divorce, yet modern English terms like "engaged" or "married" fail to capture the specific cultural reality of that time. Similarly, translating metaphors like "sheep led to the slaughter" for cultures that have no concept of sheep requires careful navigation to convey the original meaning without losing the intent.

Despite these difficulties, it is not impossible to understand the original text. The primary challenge lies in the lack of perfect English equivalents, not in the inability of the audience to comprehend the concepts. A good translation must be based on the best available manuscripts, as a translation can never be better than the source text used. Historically, the King James Version relied on the "Textus Receptus," a Greek text compiled by Erasmus using only a few late-medieval manuscripts. Since then, thousands of additional manuscripts have been discovered, some of which are over 1,000 years older than those available to Erasmus. Modern translations generally incorporate these older, more reliable sources, such as the Codex Vaticanus and the Codex Sinaiticus.

The inclusion or exclusion of certain passages, such as 1 John 5:7, illustrates the importance of textual criticism. This verse, which supports the doctrine of the Trinity, is absent from the vast majority of early Greek manuscripts and appears to have been added to the text centuries later. While some translations include it due to tradition or market pressure, scholars generally exclude it because it lacks early manuscript support. Similar issues arise with the story of the woman taken in adultery in John 7-8. These textual decisions require humility and careful explanation to congregations, as they involve complex historical evidence rather than simple dogmatism.

Ultimately, the goal of a responsible translation is to utilize the best Greek and Hebrew texts available. Discoveries like the Dead Sea Scrolls have provided Hebrew manuscripts significantly older than those previously known, allowing for greater accuracy. While the process of textual criticism is complex—involving the study of manuscript families, traditions, and the history of transmission—the objective remains to get as close to the original autographs as possible. By prioritizing older, more reliable manuscripts, translators ensure that the Word of God is presented with the highest degree of historical integrity.


## Lesson 4: Hermeneutical Issues (Part 2)

A second key requirement for a translation is that it must be based on the latest knowledge of languages and culture. Since the 1611 King James Version, our understanding of biblical poetry and linguistic interpretation has grown significantly. We must apply this modern knowledge to the text to ensure accuracy. Sometimes, translators take unnecessary liberties, such as the Living Bible’s decision to replace the Greek phrase "the sons of Zebedee" with "James and I." While the translator may have been correct about the identity of the author, an accurate translation must reflect what the text actually says rather than the translator's assumptions.

Accuracy in manuscript form does not always guarantee accuracy in print, as evidenced by several historical "notorious goofs." Printers have inadvertently omitted words, reversed letters, or introduced typos that completely altered the meaning of verses, such as the "Wicked Bible" which omitted "not" from the seventh commandment, or instances where "righteous" became "unrighteous," "sin no more" became "sin on more," and "princes" became "printers." These errors can lead to confusion or unintended humor, as seen when a congregation read "great pain" instead of "great gain" during a sermon. While accuracy is vital, it must be balanced with readability, as some highly accurate translations can be awkward to read in large sections.

A translation must also be understandable, though this sometimes conflicts with strict accuracy. The Living Bible, for instance, prioritizes clarity and orthodoxy, which makes it popular, but it often sacrifices the nuances of the original text. Furthermore, a translation must be contemporary. The English language has evolved significantly since 1611, rendering many King James terms obsolete or misleading. Words like "charity," "besom," "outlandish," "let," and "ghost" have shifted in meaning or connotation, often causing confusion for modern readers. Because language changes rapidly, translations must be periodically revised to remain relevant.

To be truly universal, a translation must account for the diverse English-speaking world. The New English Bible, while smooth and readable, is often criticized for being too British, using terminology and cultural references that do not resonate with American or other international audiences. Effective translations should involve committees of scholars from various regions—including Canada, the United States, South Africa, and Australia—to ensure that idioms and cultural markers are accessible to all English speakers rather than localized to one region.

A translation should also maintain a sense of dignity. While it should not be sanitized for political correctness, it should avoid being unnecessarily crude or shocking. Using vulgar language in a translation can distract the reader and cause the intended message to be lost, particularly among younger or more sensitive audiences. The goal is to convey the text's meaning respectfully without resorting to slang or shock tactics that undermine the gravity of the scriptures.

Finally, a translation must avoid theological bias. Some translations intentionally alter or obscure passages to support specific denominational doctrines, such as the Jehovah’s Witness Bible’s approach to the deity of Christ or the Jerusalem Bible’s handling of the perpetual virginity of Mary. When selecting a Bible, the priority should not be finding one with the "highest Christology," but rather finding one that translates Christological passages with the greatest accuracy. God does not require our help to defend His word; we should simply allow the Bible to say what it says.


## Lesson 5: Various Issues in Hermeneutics

Words that were perfectly good in translation in the past cannot be used today because language is constantly changing. This process is accelerating, meaning no translation can ever be considered final. Even if we had the original Greek and Hebrew texts and produced a perfect English translation for the year 2002, it would require updating by 2050 to communicate effectively with the people of that time. We are not asking people to convert their thinking back to the past; rather, we must convert the wording of God’s infallible word to fit the contemporary world.

When we speak of the Bible being without error, we refer to what the biblical authors originally meant. While an English translation is not inerrant in itself, it is considered without error to the extent that it faithfully reproduces the original meaning. We are concerned with even the smallest poor translations because the Bible is precious, and we will never be content with anything less than perfection, even though we know true perfection is unattainable in this life. No one reading these translations is being led astray, but one must wrestle with the philosophy of translation, specifically whether to pursue a word-for-word or a thought-for-thought approach.

Word-for-word translations, such as the King James or New American Standard, seek the nearest English equivalent for the original text. In contrast, thought-for-thought translations, like the New International Version, prioritize how best to express the author's meaning. This distinction becomes particularly complex when dealing with gendered language in the Greek, such as the word "brothers" or the use of singular male pronouns that are intended to be corporate. If the goal is to translate what the author meant rather than just the words, translators must decide how to reveal that corporate intent to a modern audience, which often leads to difficult choices regarding inclusive language.

The goal of interpretation is to understand what the biblical author meant by the words they used and how the average reader of that time would have understood them. Once that is established, the challenge is determining how to translate that meaning into modern English. Different translations serve different purposes; for example, the Revised Standard Version is excellent for scholarly comparisons of the Gospels, while other versions like the New Living Bible are better suited for casual reading or for children. We are essentially looking for ways to improve our translations, moving closer to that ideal of 100% accuracy.

Some translations, like the New Revised Standard Version, have faced criticism for allowing external pressures, such as feminist concerns, to influence the text in ways that may obscure the author's original intent. For instance, altering parables to remove male-female pairings can destroy the intentional structure of the Gospel of Luke. I generally feel more comfortable with committee-based translation projects than those done by individuals, as committees provide a necessary system of checks and balances that ensures greater reliability.

I am also wary of footnotes and study Bibles because they often carry an unearned sense of authority. When a reader encounters a footnote, they may mistakenly attribute the same infallibility to the note as they do to the biblical text itself, which can lead to confusion or misplaced reliance on human interpretation. It is important to be cautious when using these resources.

Finally, comparing different translations of a passage like Galatians 3:23-28 reveals that theological differences can sometimes emerge through translation choices. Whether a text is translated as "under the law" or "until the faith," or whether a phrase is interpreted as "through faith in Christ" versus "in Christ Jesus," can change the reader's impression of the theology. While none of these variations lead to heresy, they demonstrate that translation is an interpretive act, and it is helpful to be aware of how these subtle differences shape our understanding of the word of God.

## Lesson 8: Vocabulary for Interpretation (Part 1)

The definition of meaning is a fundamental concept that must be memorized. The meaning of a text is the pattern of meaning that the author consciously willed to convey through the words or shareable symbols they used. It is important to include the word "consciously" to distinguish the author’s intended meaning from subconscious meanings or interpretations that the author was oblivious to. In this process, the author, the text, and the reader are all present; while an author may not be aware of every implication within a pattern, those implications are still part of what was consciously willed.

Implications are those meanings within a text of which the author may have been unaware, yet they legitimately fall within the pattern of meaning the author willed. For example, when Paul wrote in Galatians 5:2 that circumcision was not necessary for salvation, he was addressing a specific conflict with Judaizers. Martin Luther later applied this same principle to the buying of indulgences. Although Paul was not thinking of indulgences, the principle of "grace alone" remains the same, making Luther’s application a legitimate implication of Paul’s original, consciously willed meaning.

This concept of implications extends to various biblical passages, such as the law of "an eye for an eye" in Exodus, which establishes a principle of proportional justice that applies beyond the specific examples of eyes or teeth. Similarly, the command in Deuteronomy 22:8 to build a parapet on a roof is not merely about ancient architecture; it establishes a principle of responsibility for the safety of others, which can be applied to modern situations like securing a swimming pool or managing a dangerous dog. The specific cultural expression of a command—such as the "holy kiss" in 1 Corinthians—may change, but the underlying principle of warm, loving concern remains constant.

When examining worship, such as in Psalm 150, the specific instruments mentioned are not an exhaustive list but rather an invitation to use the totality of available resources to praise God. Likewise, the instructions in Deuteronomy 6:6-9 regarding phylacteries and doorposts serve as a principle for keeping God’s word central in the home and in the training of children. In these cases, the author’s intent provides the framework, and the reader identifies how that principle applies to their own context.

Jesus often demonstrated this process by drawing out the implications of Old Testament scripture. When Jesus confronted the Pharisees in Mark 7, He applied Isaiah’s prophecy about hypocrites to the specific religious leaders of His day. Furthermore, when the biblical writers recorded Jesus’ teachings on the human heart, they acted as inspired interpreters, sometimes highlighting specific sins relevant to their own communities. These interpretations are valid because they remain rooted in the original, consciously willed meaning of the author.

Ultimately, the author remains in control of the implications that flow from a text. Whether it is a narrative about Jesus calming a storm or a command like the Great Commission in Acts 1:8, the text provides a pattern. While the general implications are clear, the specific application of those truths to an individual’s life—such as how one is called to be a witness—often involves the guidance of the Holy Spirit. By distinguishing between the author's consciously willed meaning and the resulting implications, one can better understand how scripture remains relevant and applicable across different cultures and generations.


## Lesson 9: Vocabulary for Interpretation (Part 2)

Significance refers to how a reader responds to the meaning of a text. While implications are determined by the author and discovered through a mental process, significance is something the reader masters. This involves the volitional aspect—the heart rather than the mind—and requires a decision regarding how to respond to the meaning. It is a matter of yes or no, obedience or disobedience.

Subject matter refers to the "stuff" talked about in the text, such as historical events, marriage customs, or military tactics. Many people study the Bible for its subject matter rather than its meaning. To find the meaning, one must ask why the author provided that specific information. Furthermore, one must distinguish between description and prescription; reading about a custom in the Bible does not necessarily mean the author is prescribing it for the reader.

Understanding is defined as the correct mental grasp of what the author intended. If two people have a correct understanding of a text, their understandings are identical, even if one is more complete than the other. Interpretation, however, is the verbal or written expression of that understanding. While understanding is mental and singular, interpretation is verbal and tends to vary among different people. The moment an understanding is expressed, it becomes an interpretation.

Mental acts are the experiences a writer goes through while writing. C.S. Lewis argued that it is impossible for readers to accurately reconstruct these mental experiences, even when the author is a contemporary. If it is impossible to reconstruct the mind of a modern author, it is even less likely that one can reconstruct the mind of a Biblical author from thousands of years ago. Therefore, the interpreter’s goal is not to guess the author's internal state, but to understand what the author meant by the words provided.

The norms of language represent the range of meanings allowed by the words or symbols of a text, which can be identified using a dictionary. Because words often have multiple possible meanings, the author provides a context to narrow these down to the norms of utterance—the specific meaning the author intended. A concordance is a helpful tool for this, as it allows the interpreter to see how the same author uses a word elsewhere.

Literary genre refers to the form used by the author and the rules that govern that form. Context is defined as the willed meaning an author gives to the literary material surrounding a text. While these definitions are precise, it is important to recognize that others may use different terminology. The goal is to translate external perspectives into this conceptual framework to maintain consistency.

Finally, while some scholars define the goal of hermeneutics as hearing the message as the original audience would have heard it, this can be problematic because audiences sometimes misunderstood the author. A more accurate goal is to determine what the author intended for the reader to understand. By focusing on the author's willed meaning through shareable symbols, the interpreter can arrive at a consistent understanding of the text.


## Lesson 10: Role of the Holy Spirit (Part 1)

For the past two lectures, we have explored the components of hermeneutics, specifically the roles of the author, the reader, and the text. I have argued that the author determines the meaning, and our task as biblical exegetes is to discern what the inspired author intended to convey through their chosen symbols and words. While we have mastered the basic vocabulary of this process, we have not yet addressed the role of the Holy Spirit.

Many scholars, such as J. Robertson McQuilken, argue that faith is a prerequisite for truly understanding Scripture. McQuilken suggests that while an unbeliever might grasp some revealed truth, they cannot fully understand it without the Holy Spirit, who is the "great Interpreter." Similarly, Millard Erickson posits that authority for the Christian is a combination of the objective written word and the subjective, inward illumination of the Holy Spirit. This dual dimension is intended to prevent both cold, sterile intellectualism and ill-advised, emotional fervor, leading instead to spiritual maturity.

The Westminster Confession of Faith also affirms that while the counsel of God is set down in Scripture, the inward illumination of the Spirit is necessary for a saving understanding. However, some modern perspectives, such as those found in James DeYoung and Sarah Hurty’s work, suggest that the Holy Spirit may reveal meanings unintended by the human author. This raises significant concerns regarding objectivity, as it becomes difficult to distinguish between the Spirit’s intended meaning and the reader’s own subjective interpretations.

This leads to the question of whether an unbeliever can understand the meaning of Scripture. If we define "meaning" as a correct mental grasp of the author's intent, we must consider whether a non-believer is capable of this. In a hypothetical classroom setting, a non-believer with the same intellectual capacity as a believer would likely be able to perform just as well on an objective exam regarding the author’s meaning. Even in practical scenarios, such as training counselors for a crusade, non-believers can accurately relay the Gospel message and guide others through the process of salvation.

The passage in 1 Corinthians 2:14, often cited to argue that unbelievers cannot understand Scripture, describes the "unspiritual man" viewing the things of the Spirit as "folly." However, the term "folly" is a value judgment rather than a cognitive inability to grasp information. Just as one must understand a subject to judge it as foolish, the unbeliever can possess a correct mental grasp of the text while simultaneously rejecting its truth. 

Ultimately, the distinction between the believer and the non-believer is not found in the ability to achieve a correct mental grasp of the words, but in the area of significance. The believer accepts the truth of the text, while the unbeliever may reject it as foolishness. As illustrated by the story of the young man who refused to repent despite having his intellectual questions answered, there is a clear difference between head knowledge and the spiritual acceptance of truth. Therefore, in our technical vocabulary, "understanding" refers to the correct mental grasp, while the work of the Holy Spirit relates to the significance and conviction that leads to a transformed life.


## Lesson 11: Role of the Holy Spirit (Part 2)

The fact of sin affects us all, particularly when we have existential involvement in texts. Unbelievers may have an axe to grind that prevents them from seeing the text clearly, but this is not one-sided; Christians can be equally prejudiced, so set in their ways that their minds are closed to any interpretation other than their own. This problem of sin works on both sides, which is why we must consider how we pray for others. When we pray for a non-Christian friend, we are not merely asking for their intellectual understanding of the Gospel, but for the Spirit of God to bring conviction and open their hearts to receive it. Similarly, in Bible studies, we often assume the meaning is clear and instead pray for the Spirit to help us see how the text applies to our lives, focusing on implications and significance rather than basic comprehension.

Regarding the interpretation of specific passages, such as Paul’s doctrine of justification by faith in Romans 4, both believers and unbelievers could hypothetically arrive at the same correct academic conclusion. Even in abstract matters, such as the command not to be drunk with wine, one can arrive at a correct understanding without special spiritual intervention. However, there are areas where the Spirit is essential, specifically regarding personal implications. While an unbeliever might understand the general command in Acts 1:8 to spread the Gospel, only the Spirit can guide a believer to understand how that command applies to their own life—perhaps calling them to a specific mission field. This personal application and the subsequent response of faith are works of the Holy Spirit.

The role of the Spirit in illumination and conviction is often misunderstood. Many equate illumination with understanding, yet this creates confusion. If we define understanding as a correct mental grasp, then an unbeliever is capable of it. If we redefine understanding to include a saving, authentic, or deeper grasp, we are simply adding qualifications to the term. It is more accurate to distinguish between a mental grasp of the text and the conviction of its truthfulness and significance. As Martin Luther noted, there is an external clarity in Scripture accessible to all, but an internal clarity that requires the Spirit of God to perceive the truth in one's heart.

Scholarly texts often reflect this tension, sometimes suggesting that only a believer can "truly understand" the Bible, while simultaneously acknowledging that unbelievers can grasp the cognitive meaning of a text. This suggests that "true understanding" is a composite of a correct mental grasp and a conviction of the text's significance. When authors speak of "new meanings" or "assimilating contents," they are often referring to this spiritual dimension of application. Ultimately, the Spirit does not necessarily provide new data, but rather enables the believer to move from an academic, mental grasp of the text to a life-changing conviction.

To illustrate this, consider the word "mammon." A group of people using a dictionary can arrive at the correct mental definition—"possessions"—just as easily as a group praying for spiritual insight. The academic meaning is accessible to anyone. However, the Spirit of God is active in moving beyond that definition to show how that truth applies to one's life, convicting the individual of the idols they must remove to serve God fully. Knowing the definition without the Spirit’s application is academic and ultimately insufficient.

In my own life, I experienced this when reflecting on John 3:16. I had always known academically that God loved the world and that I was included in that, but one day, the Spirit brought a deep, personal conviction that God truly loved me. The world seemed to change as I realized the personal implication of the text. This was not a change in my cognitive understanding of the verse, but a profound recognition in my heart. This experience highlights the major work of the Spirit in interpretation: moving us from a correct mental grasp to a deep, transformative conviction of the truth.

## Lesson 13: Implications of Genre

The Christian idea of marriage is based on Christ’s words that a man and wife are to be regarded as a single organism, a fact rather than a mere sentiment. The inventor of the human machine intended for the male and female to be totally combined, not just on a sexual level. Consequently, sexual intercourse outside of marriage is a monstrosity because it attempts to isolate one kind of union from the total union intended by God. Christianity does not condemn sexual pleasure, but it teaches that one must not isolate that pleasure from the context of the whole, just as one should not seek the pleasure of eating without the process of digestion.

Because of this total union, Christianity teaches that marriage is for life. While different Churches may have varying views on the permissibility of divorce, they all agree that divorce is a violent act, akin to a surgical operation or the loss of limbs, rather than a simple dissolution of a business partnership. They reject the modern view that marriage is a simple readjustment of partners to be made whenever people no longer feel in love or find someone else.

The lesson then transitions to the work of G.B. Caird, specifically his book *The Language and Imagery of the Bible*. Caird distinguishes between various ways the word "meaning" is used, such as meaning with respect to referent (subject matter), value (significance), entailment (implication), sense, and intension. He emphasizes that the distinction between sense and referent is indispensable, and he warns against the "intentional fallacy," which is the error of supposing that a writer meant something other than what they have actually written. We have no access to the minds of biblical authors except through their recorded words.

The discussion moves to the genres of Scripture, specifically the nature of proverbs. Interpreting the Bible requires understanding the rules of the literary forms used, much like understanding the rules of a game. A proverb is a short, pithy saying that expresses a general truth. It is not a universal law that allows for no exceptions. For example, the proverb "Train up a child in the way he should go and when he is old he will not depart from it" is a general rule, not a guarantee that every child will follow the Lord regardless of circumstances.

Misunderstanding proverbs as universal promises rather than general truths can lead to harmful applications, such as the mistakes made by Job’s comforters. They quoted true proverbs but applied them incorrectly to Job’s specific situation, effectively using them as a club to induce guilt. Biblical proverbs are formed by wise people who filter their observations of life through the lens of Scripture, providing a God-given sense of reality. While other cultures also possess proverbs, the biblical ones are uniquely informed by this divine perspective. Ultimately, proverbs are meant to be helpful guides for wise living, and recognizing them as general truths allows for the grace and nuance necessary in pastoral care.


## Lesson 14: Hermeneutics for Prophecy (Part 1)

The study of prophetic literature begins with the book of Jonah, which illustrates a fundamental rule of prophecy: when a prophet preaches judgment, the prophecy is often intended as a warning rather than an absolute prediction. If the people repent, the judgment will not take place. This principle is explicitly confirmed in Jeremiah 18:5–11, where God explains that His declarations of destruction or blessing are conditional based on the response of the nation. Jonah’s reluctance to go to Nineveh stemmed from his awareness of this rule; he knew that if he preached, the Ninevites might repent and escape the destruction he desperately wanted them to face.

This conditional nature of judgment prophecy is a recurring theme in the Old Testament, appearing in books like Micah and 1 Kings. It is important to recognize that while God is omniscient and knows the outcome, the language of "changing His mind" is an anthropomorphic way of describing His responsiveness to human repentance. From a human perspective, it appears as though God has altered His course, but in reality, the prophecy functioned as an open invitation for the people to avert disaster through a change of heart.

Beyond the conditional nature of these messages, one must also understand the specific language of prophecy, particularly the use of cosmic imagery. Passages such as Isaiah 13:9–11, which describe the sun, moon, and stars failing to give light, are often mistaken for literal descriptions of the end of the world. However, when examined in context, these passages frequently refer to the historical judgment of specific nations, such as Babylon or Egypt. By comparing these texts with other prophetic writings like Jeremiah 4, Ezekiel 32, and Amos 8, it becomes clear that this cosmic terminology is a standard feature of prophetic poetry.

The prophets utilized this symbolic, apocalyptic language to emphasize the gravity of the events they were describing. By invoking the sun, moon, and stars, the prophets were declaring that the God who controls the heavens and the earth was intervening in human history to bring judgment upon a nation. While the cosmic descriptions themselves are poetic and symbolic rather than literal, the underlying message—that God is actively bringing judgment—is a literal truth that the original readers understood perfectly. Recognizing these literary conventions is essential for correctly interpreting the prophetic genre.

## Lesson 16: Hermeneutics for Prophecy (Part 3)

Regarding the concept of *sensus plenior*, or the idea of a deeper meaning in prophecy unknown to the original prophet but understood by New Testament writers, there are two primary concerns. First, if such a meaning exists, it is only accessible after the fact through the inspiration of the New Testament writers, and it is not a methodology that we, as non-inspired readers, can reliably replicate. Second, if a passage can be interpreted as applying to the original context—such as the Day of Pentecost—then the need for a *sensus plenior* to "bail us out" of a perceived lack of relevance disappears entirely. Once we admit a passage has relevance to its original setting, the necessity for a secondary, hidden meaning becomes redundant.

When applying Old Testament passages to modern situations, such as 2 Chronicles 7:14, it is important to recognize that the text primarily addressed a specific group of people in a historical context. While one might argue for a general implication that God responds to national repentance, applying such verses flippantly to contemporary nations can be problematic. Similarly, when New Testament authors do not explicitly connect an Old Testament passage to Christ, it is safer to maintain a conservative approach. For example, Jesus’s use of Psalm 22 during the crucifixion likely reflects His personal experience and identification with the Psalmist’s suffering rather than a formal fulfillment of prophecy.

The Book of Revelation and other prophetic literature should be viewed as impressionistic art rather than a literal, high-definition photograph. Just as 19th-century impressionism requires the viewer to step back to grasp the overall image rather than focusing on individual clumps of paint, prophetic language uses emotive, figurative imagery to convey theological truths. Descriptions of Heaven or the horrors of judgment—such as the sun being darkened or the moon turning to blood—are not necessarily scientific or literal accounts. Instead, they are effective, commissive statements used to describe the magnitude of God’s intervention in history.

The interpretation of Mark 13 illustrates the complexity of this genre. While verses 24–27 describe cosmic signs that seem to point toward the Second Coming, the surrounding context—specifically the warning to flee Jerusalem and the statement that "this generation will not pass away"—strongly suggests a focus on the destruction of Jerusalem in A.D. 70. The horror of that event, as described by historians like Josephus, aligns with the intense, figurative language used by the prophets. Ultimately, the goal of interpreting prophecy is to understand the author's intent, recognizing that the original audience likely understood this figurative, impressionistic language far better than our modern, scientifically-minded generation.


## Lesson 17: Hermeneutics for Poetry (Part 1)

What we are going to talk about in regard to poetic literature will to a certain extent reinforce some of the things we have said with regard to prophecy. There is a difference between prose and poetry, and we are really very fortunate that in the Bible, in two instances, there are two accounts of the same event placed side by side. One of them is prose—narrative—and the other is poetry. Translators help us understand this difference by using a solid block form for narrative and a broken, poetic rendering for the other.

If you read chapter 4 of Judges, it is a straightforward, realistic story about Deborah and Barak leading the people of Israel against the enemy, Sisera. There is nothing in this account that seems exaggerated or figurative; it reads like a literal report. However, chapter 5 is a song—a ballad—that Deborah and Barak sang to celebrate the victory. When you read a song, you must be prepared for different kinds of language. Just as we sing songs today about trees clapping their hands or God being Lord before creation existed, we must accept that songs use poetic license to fit rhythm and rhyme, or simply to express truth in a non-literal way.

When the poets describe the victory in chapter 5, they use imagery of earthquakes and mountains quaking to describe God’s presence. This is figurative language meant to convey the certainty of God being with His people. It is not a technical military report, but a powerful, effective way to describe the Lord’s intervention. If you were to interpret these poetic descriptions as literal, physical events, you would miss the author’s intent. The author is not trying to explain the mechanics of the battle, but rather to celebrate that God was with them and gave them the victory.

This same principle applies to other passages, such as the crossing of the Red Sea. Chapter 14 provides a straightforward narrative, while chapter 15 offers a poetic song of celebration. In the song, the imagery of God throwing the army into the sea or consuming them like stubble is clearly figurative. It is a metaphor for judgment. No one is meant to take these images literally, as if the Egyptians were literally burned to death; rather, the imagery serves to illustrate the total destruction of the enemy through God’s judgment.

We see this need for understanding poetic license in the New Testament as well, such as in the hymn found in Colossians 1:15-20. The rhythmic, universal language used in such hymns can sometimes seem to conflict with other biblical teachings if pressed too literally. However, the repetition and balance required by the poetic meter often dictate the language used. Similarly, in 1 Corinthians 15:22 and Romans 5, Paul uses rhythmic, balanced statements about Adam and Christ that must be interpreted in light of the entirety of his theology, rather than being pressed into a literalism that ignores the poetic structure.

Ultimately, whether it is David singing a psalm of deliverance or a eulogy for Saul and Jonathan, the Bible uses poetry to express deep truths in ways that literal prose cannot. When David describes heroes as being "swifter than eagles" or "stronger than lions," he is using a perfectly legitimate and impressive form of expression. By recognizing these different genres, we can better understand what the authors intended to communicate, appreciating the power of their language without misinterpreting their figurative descriptions as literal, scientific, or historical reports.


## Lesson 18: Hermeneutics for Poetry (Part 2)

The lesson begins by exploring four specific kinds of poetry found in the New Testament, which originated in the Aramaic spoken by Jesus. The first type is synonymous parallelism, where the same thought is repeated in different words to reinforce a point. Examples include the Lord’s Prayer and various teachings in the Gospels. This rhythmic repetition serves as a mnemonic device, making the teachings easier to memorize and understand, which was particularly important in an essentially illiterate culture.

The second type is antithetical parallelism, where a statement is followed by its opposite to provide contrast, such as the proverb about good and bad trees. The third type, step or climactic parallelism, involves a second line that advances the thought of the first line to a higher level. Finally, chiasmic parallelism reverses the order of ideas in a second statement, creating an A-B-B-A structure. These poetic forms rely more on rhythm than rhyme, and their prevalence in the Gospels suggests that Jesus intentionally used them to ensure His teachings were retained by His followers.

The discussion then shifts to the genre of idioms, which are expressions that do not make literal sense but carry specific cultural meanings. Interpreting idioms requires understanding the author's intent rather than translating the words literally. For example, the biblical use of "hating" a person often functions as an idiom for loving someone else more, as seen in the accounts of Jacob’s wives or Jesus’ teachings on discipleship. Misinterpreting these idioms can lead to significant confusion, as literal translations often obscure the intended message.

The lesson concludes by addressing the challenges of translation and the importance of context. When translating ancient texts, one must decide whether to maintain the literal wording or translate the meaning of the idiom to avoid confusion for the modern reader. Ultimately, the meaning of a text is determined by the author's intent, and recognizing idiomatic language is essential for accurate biblical interpretation. The professor emphasizes that while literal translations have their place, thought-for-thought translations are often more effective at conveying the original message to contemporary audiences.

## Lesson 21: Hermeneutics for Parables (Part 1)

The genre of parables is broad, encompassing everything from pithy proverbs to extended metaphors. While the term "parable" in the Old Testament is quite expansive, the New Testament usage often focuses on stories, though it remains wide-ranging. To understand how to interpret these texts, it is helpful to examine the history of the Parable of the Good Samaritan, as it is one of the most famous examples. By looking at how giants of the faith—such as Augustine, Luther, and Calvin—have interpreted this passage, we can identify common mistakes and establish sound principles for modern interpretation.

The earliest known interpretation of the Good Samaritan comes from Marcion, a Gnostic who claimed the Son of God first appeared in history as the Samaritan to avoid the "evil" of physical birth. This set a precedent for allegorical interpretation, where every detail of a story is assigned a specific, hidden meaning. Clement of Alexandria and Origen further developed this into a pseudo-science, with Origen arguing that Scripture, like humanity, has a tripartite nature—body, soul, and spirit—and that the deepest spiritual truths are found only through allegory. Under this system, the man going to Jericho became Adam, the robbers were hostile influences, and the inn represented the Church.

Saint Augustine, the most influential theologian between Paul and the Reformers, solidified this allegorical approach. He interpreted the parable through a complex lens, identifying the priest and Levite as the law and prophets, and the Good Samaritan as Jesus. This method became the dominant way of interpreting sacred literature for over a millennium, largely because the early church viewed Jesus’ own explanation of the Parable of the Sower as a mandate to allegorize all parables. Furthermore, it provided a way to handle difficult or seemingly immoral passages in sacred texts by bypassing the literal meaning in favor of a spiritualized one.

During the Middle Ages, this system expanded even further, with John Cassian introducing a four-fold method of interpretation: literal, moral, spiritual, and heavenly. The Venerable Bede and Thomas Aquinas continued this tradition, with Aquinas famously justifying the four levels of meaning by comparing them to the four cardinal directions. While some might find these interpretations creative, they often strayed far from the original intent of the text.

The Reformation marked a significant turning point, as figures like Martin Luther and John Calvin rejected the allegorical method in favor of *Sola Scriptura*. Luther famously dismissed allegorizers as "clerical jugglers," and Calvin became the first major theologian to argue that the Parable of the Good Samaritan was not an allegory about Jesus at all. Calvin insisted that such speculations were "cooked up by meddlers" and lacked reverence for the actual text. Despite this, the allegorical method persisted in the interpretation of parables long after it had been abandoned for other parts of Scripture, remaining dominant well into the 19th century with scholars like R.C. Trench.

Ultimately, the history of parable interpretation serves as a warning against *eisegesis*—reading one's own ideas into the text—rather than *exegesis*, which draws the intended meaning out of the text. For those who teach and preach, the responsibility is heavy. We must strive to understand the original pattern of meaning in a passage before seeking applications for today, lest we become false witnesses by claiming to speak for God while ignoring what He actually said.


## Lesson 22: Hermeneutics for Parables (Part 2)

The new period of parable study began in 1888 with the work of Adolf Jülicher, who wrote *Die Gleichnisreden Jesu* (The Parable Talks of Jesus). In this book, Jülicher sought to distinguish between a parable and an allegory. In an allegory, such as John Bunyan’s *Pilgrim’s Progress*, the author assigns specific meanings to various details, requiring the reader to interpret those details to understand the narrative. Jülicher argued that parables are not allegories; they generally contain one basic point of comparison, while the surrounding details serve only to make the story interesting. Therefore, one should be content with identifying the main point rather than searching for hidden allegorical significance in every detail.

A parable is essentially a comparison between a picture and a reality. Because all comparisons eventually break down if pressed too far, one must avoid over-interpreting the details. For instance, in the Parable of the Good Samaritan, the specific actions taken by the Samaritan—such as using wine and oil—are simply practical first-aid measures, not symbols for theological doctrines. Adding details is an art of storytelling, not a mechanism for encoding multiple meanings. While Jülicher was a 19th-century liberal whose interpretations often reflected his own theological biases, his core contribution remains the principle that parables should not be treated as allegories.

The second major contribution to modern parable interpretation came from C.H. Dodd in 1935. Dodd emphasized that Jesus did not teach His parables to 20th-century Christians, but to 1st-century Jews. Consequently, one must investigate the "situation in life" in which the parable was uttered to understand how the original audience would have perceived it. Often, readers become so focused on applying the parable to their own lives that they lose sight of the author's original intent. By understanding the cultural and historical context, the parables become more than just abstract stories; they become powerful, living messages.

The third principle, emerging in the 1950s with Redaction Criticism, recognizes that the Gospel writers—Matthew, Mark, Luke, and John—were not merely transcribing Jesus’ words, but were inspired interpreters who emphasized specific themes. To interpret a parable correctly, one must ask what the Evangelist intended to teach. While ministers can show the implications of these teachings to a congregation, they must ensure their interpretations align with the Spirit-led message the Evangelist originally conveyed. Finally, the fourth rule is the application: determining what God wants the reader to do in response to the parable.

Applying these principles to the Parable of the Good Samaritan reveals that the main point is not about Jesus being the Savior or the return of the Messiah, but rather answering the lawyer’s question: "Who is my neighbor?" By examining the historical animosity between Jews and Samaritans, it becomes clear that Jesus was using a shocking reversal—making a Samaritan the hero—to indict the social, racial, and religious superiority of his listeners. When translated into modern contexts, such as the civil rights movement or Nazi Germany, the parable’s power to challenge and convict becomes evident. Ultimately, these parables were weapons of war in Jesus' debates, designed to provoke a radical change in perspective.


## Lesson 23: Hermeneutics for Parables (Part 3)

The lesson begins by outlining four primary rules for interpreting parables: parables are not allegories and should be understood as teaching one main point without over-analyzing details; one must seek to understand what Jesus meant by the parable; one must seek to understand what the Evangelist meant by the parable; and finally, one must identify the personal implications and significance of the teaching. Dr. Stein emphasizes that understanding a parable within its original setting often reveals it to be a disturbing or challenging message for the initial audience. He defines a parable simply as a brief or extended comparison, noting that he prefers not to get bogged down in technical subdivisions.

Dr. Stein illustrates these rules using the parable of the hidden treasure in Matthew 13:44. He explains that the story is a brief similitude meant to convey that the Kingdom of God is the greatest treasure one can possess, making it worth any cost. He warns against focusing on the character of the man in the story, arguing that the man’s morality is irrelevant to the main point. Similarly, regarding the parable of the ten maidens in Matthew 25, he cautions against obsessing over the details of the oil or the maidens' refusal to share. The central message is simply the necessity of being ready for the Lord’s return, as the details of the story are merely vehicles to convey that urgency.

The discussion moves to the parable of the dishonest steward in Luke 16, which often confuses readers due to the master’s commendation of a cheat. Dr. Stein argues that the parable is not an endorsement of dishonesty but a lesson in shrewdness regarding the imminent judgment. The steward is praised only for his foresight in preparing for his future, and the lesson for the audience is to be equally prepared for their encounter with the Lord. He connects this to the concept of stewardship, suggesting that one’s use of worldly wealth should reflect a readiness for eternal life, as illustrated by the anecdote of John D. Rockefeller’s estate.

Finally, the lesson examines the parable of the lost sheep, comparing the accounts in Luke 15 and Matthew 18. Dr. Stein notes that while the core parable likely originated with Jesus, the Evangelists adapted it to suit their specific audiences. Luke focuses on the repentance of the lost, while Matthew emphasizes the restoration of "little ones" or believers who have strayed. Dr. Stein explains that these variations are not contradictory but represent the inspired Evangelists applying the teachings of Jesus to the specific needs of their respective church communities. He concludes by clarifying that while he explores these academic nuances in class, a preacher’s role is to focus on the main point of the text to guide the congregation in their faith.

## Lesson 27: Hermeneutics for Historical Narrative (Part 1)

The lesson begins by exploring the concept of historical narrative as a genre, specifically examining how early Christians, up until the Reformation, determined if a biblical account was historically true. Dr. Stein explains that before the 18th century, the question of whether a text was "historically true" was not distinct from understanding the author's intended meaning. The early church relied on the genre of the text—such as distinguishing between parables, poetry, and historical narrative—to determine its nature. Historical narratives were inherently accepted as true, and the idea that an account could be separate from the actual event it described was a foreign concept.

Dr. Stein illustrates this shift by contrasting how a Sunday school class and a university humanities class might approach the Exodus. While both groups might read the text, the university setting introduces modern critical methods like archaeology, the study of Ancient Near Eastern literature, and scientific analysis to verify the events. This highlights the emergence of a divide between the "account" (the text) and the "event" (what actually happened). This separation became a defining issue in the 18th century, as Enlightenment rationalism led to skepticism regarding miracles, forcing scholars to reconcile their disbelief in the events with the need to find "meaningfulness" in the biblical texts.

The lecture references Hans Frei’s *The Eclipse of Biblical Narrative* to explain the polarization that occurred in hermeneutics. On one side were the supernaturalists, who maintained that the literal meaning and historical content were unified. On the other side were those who viewed the Bible as historically conditioned, leading to a two-stage process of interpretation: first, determining the literal or grammatical sense of the document (the author's intent), and second, conducting a historical assessment of the subject matter. Dr. Stein argues that this second stage is not true interpretation, but rather a separate act of historical evaluation.

Finally, the discussion turns to the crisis of finding meaning when the text and the subject matter are no longer viewed as identical. In the communicative process—consisting of the author, the text, and the reader—scholars of the 18th and 19th centuries looked for meaning either in the subject matter itself or in the author, rather than in the reader. Dr. Stein concludes by critiquing modern preaching that focuses solely on the historical event while ignoring the divinely inspired interpretation provided by the biblical author, noting that this approach often replaces the author's intended meaning with the preacher's own analysis of the event.


## Lesson 28: Hermeneutics for Historical Narrative (Part 2)

The rationalists, also known as naturalists, sought to reconstruct what literally took place in biblical accounts by stripping away the supernatural. For example, they might interpret the feeding of the 5,000 not as a miracle, but as a social event where a boy’s willingness to share inspired others to bring out their own hidden food, resulting in everyone having enough to eat. In contrast, supernaturalists believe the events occurred exactly as described, focusing on the miracle itself and the power of Jesus. However, both groups often overlook the author’s intended meaning, instead imposing their own interpretations upon the event to find significance.

Some scholars argued that the biblical authors used accommodation, meaning they knew the miracles did not happen but wrote them to teach moral values to an audience that believed in the supernatural. This theory never gained much traction because it implies the authors were dishonest, and it became clear that the evangelists sincerely believed their accounts were true. Another approach is the mythical interpretation, which suggests that stories arose from the subconscious desires and hopes of the early believing community. While this avoids accusing the authors of lying, it characterizes them as devout but ignorant, creating a difficult choice between viewing the writers as either dishonest or unintelligent.

Today, those who reject the historicity of miracles often favor the mythical approach, famously associated with David Strauss and the concept of de-mythologizing. This method seeks to uncover the underlying existential or religious truth that gave birth to the myth. While supernaturalists focus on the event itself, all these approaches—rationalist, accommodationist, and mythical—tend to reject the author’s conscious, willed meaning. This distinction is crucial, as it separates the author’s intent from the various philosophical frameworks later imposed upon the text.

When dealing with historical narrative, it is essential to interpret individual accounts in light of the entire book, much like fitting a single piece into a thousand-piece puzzle. The context of a story is the whole book, and ignoring this is often a mistake. Authors frequently provide clues to their intended meaning through editorial comments, summaries, or thematic statements. By marking these editorial insertions and observing repeated themes, a reader can discern what the author is truly trying to teach, rather than getting lost in the individual details of a specific narrative.

Thematic statements and patterns of repetition are vital tools for understanding biblical books. For instance, the book of Acts uses a geographical progression—Jerusalem, Judea, Samaria, and the ends of the earth—to structure its narrative, explaining why the focus shifts from Peter to Paul. Similarly, the book of Judges uses a recurring cycle of rebellion, retribution, repentance, and restoration, alongside the repeated refrain that "there was no king in Israel," to emphasize the need for the Davidic throne. By identifying these editorial structures, one can better grasp the author's purpose and avoid the pitfalls of misinterpreting historical narrative.

## Lesson 30: Hermeneutics for Epistles (Part 2)

When studying Greek classical literature to understand the New Testament, one encounters a massive amount of material. Rather than reading everything written in Greek, it is more effective to focus on authors who think like the Biblical writers. The Early Church Fathers, who wrote in Greek, often share a mindset closer to the Biblical authors than the classical Greek writers do. By reading works from those who think similarly to the Biblical authors, we gain better insight into the specific meanings of their words.

Another valuable resource is the Septuagint (LXX), the Greek translation of the Hebrew Old Testament. While the legend of its creation by 70 independent translators who all produced identical texts is likely a myth, the translators possessed a vocabulary and terminology that align closely with the New Testament authors. This creates a funnel-like approach to interpretation: we move from general Greek literature to the Early Church Fathers, then to the Septuagint, and finally to the Biblical authors themselves.

If we want to understand Paul, the best interpreter of Paul is Paul. By examining how he uses a specific word across his various letters, we gain significant insight. For example, in Philippians 1:29, the word translated as "granted" or "given" is derived from the root *charis*, meaning "grace." When we understand that Paul views suffering for Christ as a "gracious privilege" rather than a mere ordeal, it transforms our interpretation of the verse. This perspective is echoed by figures like Polycarp, who viewed his own martyrdom as a joy, demonstrating that the Early Church understood Paul’s unique usage of these terms.

Similarly, in Philippians 2:12, Paul’s command to "work out your own salvation" can be confusing if interpreted as earning salvation through deeds. However, by looking at how Paul uses the same Greek verb in other contexts—such as in Romans and 2 Corinthians—it becomes clear that the word refers to manifesting or carrying out something to its fulfillment, not meriting it. Paul consistently uses different terminology when discussing justification by grace versus works, confirming that he does not view this specific "working out" as an attempt to earn salvation.

This method of internal consistency also applies to understanding apparent conflicts between authors, such as the relationship between Paul and James regarding faith and works. By analyzing the semantic range of these terms, we see that they are often using the same words to describe different concepts. James describes a "dead" faith—a mere intellectual assent that even demons possess—whereas Paul describes a saving faith that works through love. Likewise, the "works" Paul rejects are those intended to earn merit or fulfill the law, while the "works" James advocates are acts of loving compassion.

Ultimately, the most effective interpretive strategy is to move from the specific to the general: start with the sentence, then the paragraph, the chapter, the book, and finally other letters by the same author. While time and historical proximity are factors, the mindset of the author is paramount. By narrowing our focus to how an author uses his own vocabulary, we avoid the pitfalls of imposing external definitions and gain a more accurate understanding of the intended meaning of the text.

## Lesson 32: Hermeneutics for Epistles (Part 4)

The next area of study involves moving from understanding a simple proposition to following a complex argument. Using Romans 13:1-7 as a model, we can trace a carefully constructed argument that functions independently of the surrounding text. Paul begins with a general exhortation to be subject to governing authorities, providing a theological ground for this command: all authority is instituted by God. Consequently, resisting authority is equivalent to resisting God’s appointment, which leads to judgment. Paul then provides a practical ground for obedience, noting that rulers are meant to be a terror to bad conduct rather than good. He explains that the state acts as God’s servant, bearing the "sword" as a symbol of the power of life and death to execute wrath on wrongdoers.

Following this, Paul provides a chiasmic summary in verse 5, linking the necessity of submission to both the avoidance of God’s wrath and the demands of conscience. He then transitions to an argument from Christian practice, specifically regarding the payment of taxes. The passage concludes with a final exhortation, mirroring the structure of the opening. When applying this to modern contexts, one must discern whether a government fulfills the divine role of rewarding good and punishing evil. While historical figures like Calvin, Luther, and Knox debated the limits of obedience to evil regimes, the core principle remains that the state’s legitimacy is tied to its function as God’s servant.

The lesson then shifts to the genre of ancient letters, which typically follow a standard form: a salutation (writer to recipient), a thanksgiving or prayer, the body of the letter, instructions or exhortations, and a conclusion. Paul’s letters often adapt this structure, such as his lengthy, "pregnant" introductions in letters to churches he did not personally found, like the church in Rome. These introductions often preview the theological arguments he intends to make, such as his specific call to apostleship and his mission to the Gentiles.

Finally, the absence of a standard element can be as significant as its presence. For example, while Paul’s letters almost always include a thanksgiving or prayer after the salutation, this section is notably missing in Galatians. This omission serves as a powerful indicator of the severity of the conflict within that church. By observing these structural patterns and deviations, students can gain deeper insight into the context and purpose of the apostolic writings. While technical study of Greek syntax and clausal relationships is a valuable tool for exegesis, the primary goal is to learn how to ask the right questions to uncover the richness of the biblical text.

The lecture then shifted to the genre of the covenant. During the late 19th and early 20th centuries, the History of Religions school sought parallels between Biblical texts and Ancient Near Eastern literature. While this research was often helpful, it occasionally led to "parallelomania," where scholars drew forced connections, such as the erroneous claim that the Christian cross originated from Mithraism. However, researchers did discover that the Hittite covenant forms from the Ancient Near East closely mirrored the structure of Biblical covenants.

There are two primary types of covenants: the Parity Covenant and the Suzerainty Covenant. A Parity Covenant is an agreement made between equals, similar to residential community rules. Conversely, a Suzerainty Covenant is a one-sided, top-down agreement dictated by a king, or "Suzerain," to his subjects. These covenants were gracious, but they were not negotiated; the king established the terms, and the people accepted them. This structure is essential to understanding the Biblical relationship between God and His people.

Although many Baptist traditions do not emphasize the concept of covenants, the Bible is framed by them. In Genesis 17, God establishes a covenant with Abraham, unilaterally dictating the terms and promising to be God to him and his offspring. This covenant is later remembered in Exodus when God hears the cries of the Israelites in bondage. The New Testament continues this theme, as seen in Mark 14, where Jesus refers to the cup as the "New Covenant in my blood," and in Acts 3, where Peter connects the Gospel message to the covenant God made with the ancestors of Israel.

A typical covenant form, such as the one renewed at Mount Sinai in Exodus 20, includes a preamble where the covenant maker identifies himself, followed by a historical prologue describing the gracious acts performed for the people. The stipulations, such as the Ten Commandments, follow these sections. Crucially, these stipulations are not requirements to enter the covenant, but rather instructions for remaining faithful within a relationship that has already been graciously established. The covenant concludes with provisions for its continual reading, ensuring the people remain mindful of their identity and obligations.


## Lesson 34: Genre of Covenant (Part 2)

There is oftentimes reference to various witnesses that are present. In verses 3 and 7 of the passage regarding Moses, there is a witness to what is going on. Sometimes a stone or a memorial is erected so that whenever the people look at it or pass by, they will remember the covenant that God graciously made with them. While not always as explicit as in other instances, there are frequently blessings and curses associated with the covenant, such as the promise that God blesses those who are faithful for a thousand generations but will not acquit those who disobey. The people then make an oath to obey the covenant, which serves as the covenantal form.

A larger example of this is found in Joshua 24, which contains a preamble, an extensive historical prologue, stipulations, and witnesses. The historical prologue, spanning verses 2b through 13, recounts God’s gracious acts toward Israel, such as leading Abraham, the exodus from Egypt, and the defeat of their enemies. These events occurred before the stipulations were given, demonstrating that God’s grace is not based on the people's performance. Following this, verses 14 through 21 outline the stipulations, calling the people to serve the Lord in sincerity and faithfulness. The people affirm their commitment, and Joshua establishes witnesses—including a large stone—to testify to their agreement.

Understanding this covenantal form is a vital hermeneutical tool. It clarifies that Old Testament commandments are not legalism, but rather stipulations for those who have already graciously entered into a relationship with God. These laws guide the community within the covenant, and while they are not the means of entering the covenant, disobedience carries the consequence of being cast out. When discussing these concepts, it is important to avoid forcing the biblical text into a rigid theological system. Instead, one should wrestle with the text and recognize that God’s covenants are inherently gracious acts of love.

Regarding the law, a distinction exists between cultic, civil, and ethical regulations. The New Testament suggests that cultic laws, such as dietary restrictions, have reached their fulfillment in Christ and are no longer binding. Ethical laws, however, reflect the character of God and remain relevant, while civil laws provide wisdom for how a society might function justly. Ultimately, the law serves as a way for those in a relationship with God to know how to please Him. When Christians sin, asking for forgiveness is not a matter of being "resaved," but rather a way to restore the fellowship and intimacy of the relationship with their Heavenly Father.


## Lesson 35: Genre of Psalms

The book of Psalms is the largest in the Bible and is organized into five distinct sections, each concluding with a doxology. Book 1 (Psalms 1–41) ends with a blessing to the Lord, God of Israel. Book 2 (Psalms 42–72) concludes with a doxology and a note that the prayers of David, son of Jesse, are ended. Book 3 (Psalms 73–89) is a shorter section, while Book 4 (Psalms 90–106) and Book 5 (Psalms 107–150) follow, with Psalm 150 serving as a grand, final doxology for the entire collection.

Regarding authorship, approximately 73 psalms are attributed to David, accounting for about half of the total. Other significant contributors include Asaph and the sons of Korah. Together, these three sources account for roughly two-thirds of the book. The editorial arrangement of these psalms is considered divinely inspired, as the placement and organization of the texts are essential for proper interpretation.

The Psalms are categorized into various types, each serving a different purpose in the life of the believer. Psalms of Zion, such as 48, 46, 76, 87, and 122, celebrate God’s presence in the city of Jerusalem. Royal Psalms, including 2, 21, 45, 72, and 89, focus on the king of Israel. While these were originally sung for David’s successors, they carry prophetic implications that New Testament writers often apply to the Lord Jesus, the ultimate King of kings.

Other categories include hymns of praise, such as Psalm 100, and Wisdom Psalms, like 1, 37, and 127, which offer guidance for living. Penitential Psalms, such as 6, 32, 51, and 130, provide a framework for confessing sin and seeking forgiveness. These psalms are particularly powerful for those who have felt the weight of their own transgressions and found relief through God’s mercy.

The imprecatory Psalms, including 35, 58, 69, 83, 109, and 137, are the most difficult to process. Rather than being expressions of personal vengeance, these psalms represent a cry for God’s justice to be enacted against brutal evil. They reflect a desire for righteousness to prevail, acknowledging that God, as the supreme Judge, must ultimately deal with wickedness.

Lament Psalms make up nearly one-third of the collection, with 48 psalms falling into this category. They typically follow a structure of address, description of need, prayer for help, confession of confidence, and a vow of praise. Despite the deep anguish expressed in these prayers, they consistently move toward hope. These psalms are vital for the church, as they provide a necessary outlet for believers to express their pain, confusion, and need for God during life’s most difficult seasons.


## Lesson 36: Hermeneutics and the Canon of Scripture (Part 1)

The lesson began with a discussion on the Canon of Scripture, noting that by the time of Jesus, the Old Testament was already defined as the Law, the Prophets, and the Writings. The numbering of these books varied, with the Jewish tradition grouping the Minor Prophets as one book, leading to a different total count than the 39 books found in the Protestant Canon. The Apocrypha, which is included in the Catholic Bible but not the Jewish Canon, was addressed, noting that the New Testament writers never quoted these books as authoritative Scripture, nor did they view them as part of the Canon. While these books offer valuable insight into Jewish piety, they also contain historical errors and elements of magic, distinguishing them from the established Canon.

The Council of Jamnia in AD 90 was clarified as a point of discussion regarding specific books like Esther, rather than the initial establishment of the Old Testament Canon. Regarding the New Testament, the early Church relied on the Old Testament and the oral traditions of Jesus’ life and teachings. These traditions were eventually inscripturated into the four Gospels. The timeline for the writing of the New Testament books was reviewed, noting that Paul’s letters were written between AD 50 and 65, while the Gospels and other epistles followed, with the process of canonization being driven by factors such as the rise of Marcion’s heresy, the development of the codex, and the persecution of the Church, which forced believers to identify which writings were worth dying for.

Evidence of the emerging New Testament Canon is found within the texts themselves, such as in Colossians 4:16 and 1 Thessalonians 5:27, where Paul instructs that his letters be exchanged and read among different churches. A significant reference occurs in 2 Peter 3:16, where Paul’s letters are explicitly equated with "the other Scriptures." Similarly, 1 Timothy 5:18 quotes a saying of Jesus alongside a verse from Deuteronomy, labeling both as "Scripture." While some scholars debate whether this refers to a written Gospel or oral tradition, it demonstrates that the words of Jesus were held in the same authoritative regard as the Old Testament.

Early Church Fathers and documents further illustrate the solidification of the Canon. 1st Clement, the Didache, and the writings of Papias show that by the early 2nd century, the Gospels and Pauline letters were being cited as Scripture. By 170 AD, Tatian’s *Diatesseron* combined the four Gospels into a single narrative, confirming their status as the authoritative four. The Muratorian Canon, dating to the end of the 2nd century, provides a list of books accepted by the universal Church, which closely mirrors the modern New Testament.

By 200 AD, church leaders like Irenaeus categorized these writings into the *homolegoumena* (universally confessed) and the *antilegomena* (disputed). This classification was later refined by the historian Eusebius in 325 AD, who distinguished between recognized books, disputed books that were nonetheless known to most, and those considered non-genuine. The Book of Revelation, in particular, faced polarized reception due to its association with extremist groups like the Montanists, leading to a "hot or cold" attitude toward its inclusion in the Canon. Ultimately, these historical developments show that the Church’s recognition of the Canon was a gradual process of identifying the writings that held the authority of the Word of God.
