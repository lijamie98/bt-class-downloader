Welcome to chapter six, where we begin our study of the nominative and accusative cases, which are central to the Greek language. A typical Greek noun consists of a stem, which carries the word's meaning, and a case ending appended to the end. While the speaker chooses a case ending based on the intended function, we as translators perform the reverse process: we identify the case ending to determine the word's function.

The choice of a case ending is influenced by several factors, including the word's gender, number, and declension. Similarly, the number—whether singular or plural—and the specific declension pattern of the noun dictate which ending is appropriate. Because these factors interact, you must learn to hold multiple pieces of information in your memory simultaneously to correctly parse a word.

The nominative case is roughly equivalent to the subject of an English sentence. When a speaker wants to indicate that a word is the subject of a verb, they append the appropriate nominative case ending. Greek relies on inflection rather than word order, so we must train ourselves to scan for nominative endings to find the subject rather than assuming the first word in a sentence holds that role.

The accusative case functions similarly to the English objective case, marking a word as the direct object of a verb. Just as with the nominative, the speaker selects the appropriate accusative ending based on the noun's gender and declension. ## Lesson 10: Third Declension

The third declension is the final declension to learn, and it is defined by stems that end in a consonant. While the first and second declensions end in vowels, the third declension follows a different pattern based on its consonant stems. It is important to remember that these distinctions are purely matters of form rather than meaning; a word’s declension does not change its definition. By focusing on the true case endings rather than memorizing final stem vowels, you can avoid the common difficulties associated with this declension. For example, the word for flesh, "sark," adds a sigma in the nominative singular, resulting in "sarx" because the kappa and sigma combine to form the letter xi.

To master the third declension, you should follow four key hints. First, always memorize the genitive singular form along with the nominative, as dropping the genitive ending reveals the word's true root. Second, whatever phonetic change occurs in the nominative singular also occurs in the dative plural, as both cases involve a sigma. Third, when a nu is followed by a sigma, the nu drops out for pronunciation. Fourth, if a tau or a word ending in a tau meets a sigma, the tau drops out. Understanding these rules allows you to navigate the paradigms without needing to memorize every single variation individually.

The third declension introduces only three truly new case endings, while the others are variations of what you have already learned. The nominative singular and genitive singular are easily identified if you have memorized the lexical forms. The dative singular remains consistent with previous declensions, though it lacks a subscript because it attaches to a consonant rather than a vowel. The accusative singular and nominative plural provide the primary new endings to memorize. By applying the phonetic rules—such as the behavior of the sigma and the dropping of certain consonants—you can successfully deconstruct and understand complex inflected forms.

Finally, the "Square Stops" chart is a critical tool for understanding how consonants interact with endings. Consonants are categorized into labials (pi, beta, phi), velars (kappa, gamma, chi), and dentals (tau, delta, theta). These groups behave consistently; for instance, any labial followed by a sigma will result in a psi, while any velar followed by a sigma results in a xi. Dentals simply drop out when they encounter a sigma. Mastering this classification system, along with the final rule regarding the dropping of final taus, provides the necessary framework to handle third declension nouns and verbs throughout the Greek language.

## Lesson 25: Perfect Indicative

You have reached chapter 25, where you will learn the final tenses: the perfect active and the perfect middle passive. To understand the perfect tense, consider the difference between "I wrote," "I have written," and "it is written." "I wrote" describes a simple past action. "I have written" describes an action completed in the past that has ongoing consequences or implications in the present. "It is written" also describes a past completed action, but the emphasis shifts entirely to those ongoing implications. The Greek perfect tense functions exactly this way, describing a completed action with present relevance. In first-year Greek, we generally translate the perfect tense using the helping verbs "have" or "has," though "it is written" may eventually become a standard translation for certain perfect forms.

To form the perfect active, you begin with reduplication, followed by the perfect active tense stem and the tense formative. If it is a first perfect, the tense formative is kappa-alpha; if it is a second perfect, it is simply alpha. Because it is not an augmented tense, you use primary active personal endings. Reduplication involves repeating the initial consonant with an epsilon in between for stems beginning with a consonant, or using vocalic reduplication (which looks like an augment but indicates completed action) for stems beginning with a vowel. The perfect active is generally easy to recognize because the reduplication is unique to this tense.
