<h2 id="lesson-2-learning-greek">Lesson 2: Learning Greek</h2>

### I. Importance of Memorization

In chapter two, entitled "Learning Greek," I want to share the kinds of things I would share in a traditional classroom. Everyone has different hurdles to overcome; some memorize easily but struggle with conceptual understanding, while others struggle with vocabulary. Memorization is a common hurdle, and one of the hallmarks of this textbook is that I have tried to reduce the amount of memorization as much as possible. Because learning Greek was harder for me than for most of my colleagues, I wrote a different kind of textbook to help students navigate these challenges.

#### A. Reducing Memorization in the Textbook

I have worked hard to save you a lot of memory work. I am not especially strong in languages, so I designed the textbook to minimize the burden of memorization while acknowledging that you still have to practice and put in the work.

#### B. Tools to Help Memorize

There are several tools available to assist you in this process.

##### 1. Flashcards
Get flashcards, either the ones I made or by cutting three-by-five index cards into thirds. Put the Greek on one side and the English on the other. Carrying them with you allows you to review difficult words whenever you have a moment.

##### 2. FlashWorks Software
If you prefer electronics, I encourage you to use my software program, FlashWorks. It is free to download from BillMoyers.com and includes smart features that allow you to customize your review schedule based on which words you know well and which ones you need to practice more often.

##### 3. Online Website
Make sure to use the online website at BillMoyers.com. The basic class is free and includes vocabulary tools, audio pronunciations in both Erasmian and modern Greek, mnemonic devices, and various ways to practice. I am also doing a "Greek Word of the Day" sequence to provide further support.

### II. Doing Exercises

Be sure to do the exercises. The textbook provides theoretical knowledge, but you will not process the information until you put pen to paper. My encouragement is to read the chapter, shut the textbook, and do as many exercises as you can. If you cannot get through them, review the material, shut the book again, and treat the exercises like a test.

### III. Time and Consistency

There is no substitute for time and consistency. You cannot cram Greek; it takes time for the information to soak in.

#### A. No Substitute for Time

You may feel uncomfortable with a chapter, but you should move on to the next one anyway. Once you are a few chapters ahead, you will look back and realize the earlier material has become easier because you allowed it time to sink in. Do not get stuck trying to get every little thing perfect before moving forward.

#### B. Consistency in Studying

Keep your pace consistent and dedicate a regular amount of time each day to your studies. It is also helpful to have a consistent location where you can study and speak the language out loud. Whether you are an external processor or not, finding a dedicated space and time will help you maintain the discipline required to learn.

### IV. Discipline and Remembering the Goal

At the end of the day, learning Greek is about discipline. To stay disciplined, constantly remember your goal. Hopefully, you are doing this because you want to know Jesus better. If you always rely on a translation, you are one step removed from the original text. Just as one would not study German literature without knowing German, you should want to read the actual words God wrote. Keep this goal in mind to inform your discipline and encourage you to immerse yourself in the language—see it, write it, listen to it, and say it out loud. Being a pastor is about being a herald of the King, and being able to articulate the Word of God clearly in Greek is an important tool for that calling. Welcome to the journey of learning God's language.

<h2 id="lesson-21-imperfect-indicative">Lesson 21: Imperfect Indicative</h2>

### I. Introduction to Imperfect Indicative

#### A. Definition and Usage
The imperfect tense is used to describe a continuous action, normally in past time. While the present tense can be either continuous or undefined, the imperfect is always continuous. Greek possesses another past tense used to describe undefined actions, but the imperfect remains strictly for continuous past action.

#### B. Formation
The imperfect is formed from four basic parts: an augment, the present tense stem, a connecting vowel, and secondary personal endings. The augment is a prefix attached to the beginning of the verb to indicate that it is a secondary tense in past time.

### II. Regular Verbs in the Imperfect Indicative

#### A. First Conjugation
When the present tense stem begins with a consonant, the augment is an epsilon. For example, taking the stem "lou," adding the epsilon augment, a connecting vowel, and the secondary personal ending "nu" results in "eluon," meaning "I was loosing" or "I was destroying."

#### B. Second Conjugation
If the present tense stem begins with a vowel, the augment lengthens that initial vowel. For instance, an alpha lengthens to an eta, as seen in "agapao" becoming "egapon." Similarly, an epsilon lengthens to an eta, such as "erchomai" becoming "erchomen."

#### C. Third Conjugation
For compound verbs, the augment is applied to the verbal part of the verb rather than the preposition. Typically, if the preposition ends in a vowel, that vowel is dropped before the augment. If the preposition ends in an iota, the iota often remains, but the verbal root still receives the augment.

### III. Irregular Verbs in the Imperfect Indicative

#### A. Contract Verbs
Initial diphthongs generally show no change when augmented. While an initial epsilon might sometimes go to an eta, most initial diphthongs remain as they are without showing the augment.

#### B. Liquid Verbs
The master personal ending chart consists of four quadrants: primary active (upper left), primary middle-passive (lower left), secondary active (upper right), and secondary middle-passive (lower right). The imperfect active uses the secondary active endings (nu, sigma, nothing, men, te, nu), while the imperfect middle-passive uses the secondary middle-passive endings (men, sou, to, metha, sthe, nto).

### IV. Translation and Interpretation of the Imperfect Indicative

#### A. Contextual Clues
In the secondary active endings, the first singular and third plural forms are identical. While this may seem confusing, context usually provides the necessary indication of whether the subject is "I" or "they."

#### B. Aspect and Aktionsart
The imperfect indicates continuous action in past time. The middle and passive forms are identical in the imperfect, just as they are in the present tense.

#### C. Common Errors and Misunderstandings
Students must be careful to keep up with master verb memorization. Procrastination leads to an unmanageable workload. It is critical to commit the master personal ending chart to absolute memory, as every subsequent tense will rely on these endings or slight variations thereof.

<h2 id="lesson-25-perfect-indicative">Lesson 25: Perfect Indicative</h2>

### I. Introduction to the Perfect Indicative

#### A. Definition
The perfect tense describes a completed action that took place in the past, but it is an action that has ongoing consequences, implications, or is still true at the time of speaking. While the aorist describes a simple past action, the perfect emphasizes the state resulting from that completed action.

#### B. Usage
In first-year Greek, we generally reserve the helping verbs "have" or "has" to translate the perfect tense (e.g., "I have written"). As you become more comfortable with the language, you may look at the consequences of a verse and translate it as "it is written," which emphasizes the ongoing implications of the past event.

### II. Forming the Perfect Indicative

#### A. Perfect Active
To form the perfect active, you start with reduplication, followed by the perfect active tense stem. The tense formative for the first perfect is kappa-alpha, while the second perfect uses just alpha. Because it is not an augmented tense, you use primary active personal endings.

#### B. Perfect Middle/Passive
The perfect middle/passive uses the fifth tense stem and primary passive personal endings. Notably, there is no connecting vowel and no tense formative. If you recognize that there is no connecting vowel or tense formative, it must be a perfect middle/passive.

##### 1. Reduplication
There are two kinds of reduplication. If the stem begins with a consonant, you undergo consonantal reduplication: the initial consonant duplicates and an epsilon is placed in between (e.g., *lu* becomes *le-lu-ka*). If the stem begins with a vowel, it undergoes vocalic reduplication, which looks like an augment but indicates completed action rather than past time.

##### 2. Augment
It is important to note that vocalic reduplication is not the same thing as an augment. An augment indicates past time, whereas reduplication is a marker of the perfect tense indicating completed action.

### III. Conjugating the Perfect Indicative

#### A. First and Second Conjugation
The perfect active paradigm (e.g., *le-lu-ka, le-lu-kas, le-lu-ke*) shares similarities with the first aorist, though it uses a kappa instead of a sigma. The third plural ending is *le-lu-kasi*. The perfect middle/passive (e.g., *le-lu-mai, le-lu-sai, le-lu-tai*) is straightforward, and the second person singular ending *sai* finally appears here.

#### B. Third Conjugation
It is imperative to work through all the different tense forms and update your vocabulary cards. You must review the chart in the back of BBG that lists words occurring 50 times or more with their various verb stems. Remember that the four categories of verbs—those ending in iota and upsilon, contract stems, liquid stems, and stop stems—form all their different tense stems very regularly.

### IV. Translation Tips and Challenges

#### A. Context
Seventy-five percent of all middle forms in the New Testament are deponent. Of the remaining 25%, many have meanings very close to the active voice. However, a true middle describes an action that comes back and affects the subject. While we often translate these as reflexive (e.g., "I lose for myself"), Koine Greek usually uses a reflexive pronoun with an active verb to express this. If you see a form that is not deponent and does not make sense as a passive, consider if it is a middle.

#### B. Verbs with Irregular Forms
You must determine if you recognize an inflected form or if you have to memorize it. Use markers to distinguish between forms you can identify through rules and those that require rote memorization. <h2 id="lesson-27-present-continuous-adverbial-participles">Lesson 27: Present (Continuous) Adverbial Participles</h2>

### I. Introduction to Present Continuous Adverbial Participles

#### A. Definition and Function
In chapter 27, we are going to take the grammar learned in chapter 26 and apply it to a specific kind of participle. Participles break into two basic categories: adjectival or adverbial. If a participle is adverbial, its primary meaning is helping you to understand something about the verb. If it is functioning adjectivally, it tells you something about a noun. They are identical in form, so we must learn when they are functioning adverbially versus adjectivally. In this chapter, we are only dealing with participles built on the present tense stem, which means they are always continuous in aspect. Furthermore, all the participles we are looking at in this chapter are categorized as adverbial.

#### B. Importance in Biblical Greek
Even though an adverbial participle modifies the verb, it still must agree with a noun in case, number, and gender. If the subject is doing the participle, the participle will match the subject; if the direct object is doing it, it will match the object. While the majority of adverbial participles are nominative because the subject of the verb is usually the one performing the participle, this is not always the case. The big clue when reading the New Testament is that all adverbial participles must be anarthrous; it is impossible to have an adverbial participle with an article.

### II. Identifying Present Continuous Adverbial Participles

#### A. Morphology and Syntax
To form the present or continuous participle, you start with the present tense stem, add a connecting vowel, the appropriate participle morpheme, and the case endings. For the active voice, masculine and neuter use the morpheme *nt*, while the feminine uses *ousa*. For middle/passive, it is *men* with the appropriate vowel. In the nominative and accusative singular masculine, the case ending drops, the tau drops, and the omicron lengthens to omega. The feminine forms are regular, following an alpha-eta shift.

#### B. Context Clues
The plural forms are straightforward, though in the masculine and neuter, they become third declension. The nu-tau drops out before the case ending sigma-iota, and the vowel lengthens to compensate. You need to be aware that these can look like third-person plural indicatives, but context makes them easy to distinguish. For memorization, the key is to learn the nominative and genitive singular for all three genders. If you memorize those six forms, the rest of the paradigm flows.

### III. Translating Present Continuous Adverbial Participles

#### A. Handling Time and Aspect
The challenge in translating participles is that you must look at several things—aspect, voice, and the meaning of the word—and then determine how to express that in English. Since they are all continuous, it is often best initially to write in a way that makes the continuous aspect clear, perhaps using the word "continual." The simplest method is to add "-ing" to the verb.

#### B. Managing Sentence Structure
With adverbial participles, you can sometimes turn them into temporal clauses by adding the word "while," or causal clauses by adding "because." As you progress in Greek, you will find there are many more keywords that can be used if they fit the context and create a better flow of English.

### IV. Applications in New Testament Exegesis

#### A. Examples from Biblical Texts
For example, in the phrase "while eating, she walked into the room," the author wants to tell you something about the walking while simultaneously describing what was happening at that time. The thrust of the participle is directed against the verb, but it still agrees with a noun to get its case, number, and gender.

#### B. Insights for Interpretation
Choosing the right translation—whether using "-ing," "while," or "because"—is part of the interpretation aspect of Greek. You must hold the different grammatical elements together and ask yourself how to best convey the meaning in English to make good sense of the text.

<h2 id="lesson-30-perfect-participles-and-genitive-absolutes">Lesson 30: Perfect Participles and Genitive Absolutes</h2>

### I. Perfect Participles

#### A. Formation and Usage

##### 1. Active Voice
Today we are looking at perfect participles, which are used to indicate completed action, just like the perfect does in the indicative. To identify the perfect active participle, you look for reduplication, which signals you are in the perfect system. You have a tense formative in a kappa, followed by the perfect active participle morpheme, which is omicron-tau. Because the tau is a consonant, it takes third declension endings. For example, *lelukotes* is a perfect active participle, nominative plural, masculine.

##### 2. Middle Voice
The perfect middle passive participle is formed with reduplication, but you will notice there are no tense formatives and no connecting vowels; that is the big sign of the perfect middle passive. The participle morpheme is *menos* or *mene*.

##### 3. Passive Voice
The perfect middle passive participle uses the same morpheme as the middle, *menos* or *mene*, and takes either second or first declension case endings. For example, *lelumenos* is a perfect middle passive participle, nominative singular, masculine. When you have a perfect stem that lengthens the initial vowel as vocalic reduplication to indicate the perfect, that long vowel remains in the non-indicative because it is not indicating past time.

#### B. Translation Techniques

##### 1. Perfect Tense
As a general rule, you will use "after" and "having" to translate these. You are still using "have" as you do in the indicative, but because it is a participle, you add the "-ing" suffix. For example, *lelukotes* would be translated as "after having saved."

##### 2. Pluperfect Tense
If the participle were passive, you would translate it as "after having been saved." This is more a matter of English construction than anything else.

### II. Genitive Absolutes

#### A. Definition and Function
A genitive absolute is a construction consisting of a noun or a pronoun and a participle in the genitive case that are not grammatically connected to anything else in the sentence. There is nothing for the noun or pronoun of the participle to modify. They are used when the writer wants to describe an action being done by someone or something other than the subject of the main sentence. They often appear at the beginning of sentences in narrative material.

#### B. Formation

##### 1. Noun and Participle Agreement
The noun or pronoun in the genitive acts as if it is the subject of the participle. While participles technically cannot take subjects, in a genitive absolute, the genitive noun or pronoun functions in that capacity.

##### 2. Tense and Voice of the Participle
The participle will appear in the genitive case, matching the noun or pronoun. If there is no noun or pronoun present, the participle simply stands alone in the genitive.

#### C. Translation Techniques

##### 1. Contextual Clues
Genitive absolutes are idiomatic and cannot be translated word-for-word. They are generally temporal, so you can use "while" or "after" followed by an "-ing" form of the participle. If the subject of the participle is expressed, you should generally turn the participle into a regular verb. For example, if you have a noun in the genitive, you might translate it as "after Jesus had been born."

##### 2. Temporal or Circumstantial Relationships
If there is no noun or pronoun in the genitive, you simply use the "-ing" form of the participle, such as "after having been born." Additionally, Greek uses a periphrastic construction—a roundabout way of saying something—by joining a form of *eimi* with a participle to emphasize continuous action. While this was originally used to stress linear or ongoing action, over time that emphasis has been lost, so you must look at the context to determine if the continuous nature of the verb is being stressed.

<h2 id="lesson-31-subjunctive-mood">Lesson 31: Subjunctive Mood</h2>

### I. Introduction to the Subjunctive Mood

#### A. Definition and Usage
The indicative mood is the mood of reality, used for statements of fact, assertions, and questions. It represents what is. In contrast, the subjunctive is the mood of probability or possibility, representing what may or might be. It takes one step back from reality. For example, while the indicative says "I am rich," the subjunctive expresses the hypothetical, such as "If I were a rich man."

#### B. Importance in Biblical Greek
Understanding the subjunctive is essential for moving beyond the indicative system. Because the subjunctive is not concerned with absolute time, it focuses on aspect—either continuous or undefined—rather than tense in the traditional sense.

### II. Formation of the Subjunctive Mood

#### A. Present Subjunctive
The present subjunctive is formed using the present tense stem. It is characterized by a lengthened connecting vowel: omicrons become omegas, and epsilons become etas. These forms use the same personal endings as the present indicative. When translating, one may use "may" or "might," or reflect the continuous aspect by saying "we continue to loose."

#### B. Aorist Subjunctive

##### 1. First Aorist
The first aorist subjunctive is formed on the aorist stem. It lacks an augment because it is outside the indicative system. It uses the same personal endings as the present, but it does not use a tense formative sigma if it is not followed by an alpha.

##### 2. Second Aorist
Second aorist subjunctives also lack an augment and a tense formative. Identifying these requires memory work; if a form does not match a known present tense stem, one must look for the aorist stem.

### III. Functions of the Subjunctive Mood

#### A. Purpose Clauses
Certain words, such as *hina* (in order that), trigger the subjunctive. When a purpose clause is introduced, the verb must shift into the subjunctive because it is no longer describing a reality, but a purpose or goal.

#### B. Prohibition
The particle *me* is a major red flag. While *ou* negates the indicative, *me* is used to negate non-indicative forms, including the subjunctive, participles, infinitives, and imperatives.

#### C. Deliberative Questions
The deliberative subjunctive is used when a speaker asks a question not for a simple yes or no answer, but to invite the listener to deliberate or think about the matter.

#### D. Conditional Statements
Conditional sentences, particularly those starting with *ean*, often use the subjunctive in the "if" clause to describe something that may or might happen.

#### E. Hortatory Subjunctive
(Note: While mentioned in the outline, the transcript focuses on the four ways to ask questions and the use of *ou* and *me* to indicate expected answers.) The use of *ou* at the beginning of a question indicates the author expects a "yes" answer, while *me* indicates the author expects a "no" answer.

### IV. Tips for Identifying and Translating Subjunctive Mood

#### A. Recognizing Subjunctive Verbs
The primary formal clue is the lengthened connecting vowel. Additionally, one must be aware that there is no such thing as a future subjunctive; if a form looks like a future but lacks the sigma or has a lengthened vowel, it is likely a subjunctive.

#### B. Contextual Clues
Watch for trigger words like *hina*, *ean*, or the particle *me*. These words signal that the verb following them is likely in the subjunctive mood. Recognizing these triggers is often easier and more reliable than relying solely on identifying the long vowel in the verb form.

### I. Introduction to the Imperative Mood

#### A. Definition and Usage
The imperative is the mood of command. If you want to tell someone to do something, the verb goes into the imperative. ### I. Introduction to Odds and Ends in Biblical Greek

#### A. Purpose and Goals

Welcome to chapter 35. In chapter 34, we concentrated on *didōmi* and its indicative forms. In this chapter, we are looking at the other categories of *mi* verbs and some of the non-indicative forms of *didōmi*. We are essentially piecemeal picking up the rest of the thematic conjugation. *Mi* verbs are categorized on the basis of the final stem vowel, just like contract verbs. What you will find is that the *mi* verbs in the other classes behave just like *didōmi* does, except for the differences of the vowel.

### II. Specific Greek Constructs and Their Importance

#### A. Conditional Sentences

If you look at *tithēmi*, it is a representative *mi* verb whose root ends in an epsilon. It is formed from its root in the same way *didōmi* is, with one difference: instead of a duplicated theta, the Greeks turn the first theta into a tau to avoid the necessary pause required to say a duplicated theta. This is a characteristic of stems beginning with a theta. You can see the lengthened root vowel—where *didōmi* has an omicron going to an omega, *tithēmi* has an epsilon going to an eta. If you see the theta, the long vowel, and the reduplication with the tau and the iota, that tells you it is the present tense system.

#### B. Greek Particles

When you look at the alpha-class *mi* verbs, you have the root *sta-*. The twist here is that when the sigma gets reduplicated, the Greeks swap it with a rough breathing. So, *sta-* goes to *histēmi*. In a sense, think of that rough breathing as a reduplicated sigma. Alpha-class *mi* verbs are pretty straightforward once you understand the reduplication and the rough breathing. *Deiknymi* is the only *mi* verb you are going to learn that is in the process of wanting to become a thematic verb; it has no reduplication, though it uses the same endings.

### III. Parsing and Translating Difficult Passages

#### A. Techniques and Approaches

To summarize, in *histēmi* you have the reduplicated sigma becoming a rough breathing, in *tithēmi* you have the reduplicated theta becoming a tau, and in *deiknymi* you have a verb trying to change its conjugation. Other than that, they are perfectly regular. If you get *didōmi* firmly in your head, know the roots of the other *mi* verbs, and understand the peculiarities of those verbs, the *mi* verb system falls into place.
